# Alphas - The Beginning
## Chapter 3: The Dead Man's Switch

Frank sat at the corner table of the coffee shop, his fingers drumming a restless rhythm against the wood. He had rounded up the others within minutes of Amanda's message, though a part of him was still grumbling internally about the important lecture he was currently missing. Around the table, the atmosphere was thick with a mixture of confusion and growing apprehension.

William leaned forward, his voice lowered but sharp with disbelief. He looked at Frank imploringly. "So, just to be clear... we are actually helping a murderer?"

"Suspect," Peter corrected instantly, his tone level and objective. He leaned back in his chair, his gaze steady. "Legally and ethically, there is a massive difference, William. Don't let a font size do your thinking for you."

"The headline didn't look like it left much room for interpretation," William hissed back, glancing nervously at the door as if expecting a SWAT team to burst in.

"I need you both to calm down right now and trust Amanda," Frank said, his voice dropping into that authoritative tone that usually silenced their bickering. He checked his watch, then looked back at the door. "She wouldn't have called us here if she wasn't certain. If she says there's more to this, then we stay put and we listen. Clear?"

The bell above the door chimed, cutting through the low hum of the espresso machine. The small group went silent, their heads turning in unison as two figures stepped out of the cold and into the warmth of the shop.

Amanda walked in first, her posture defensive and protective. Following a half-step behind her was Idris, his hood pulled down, his face pale and eyes wary. He looked less like the "cold-blooded killer" from the college site and more like a man who had been walking through a storm for a very long time.

Idris looked at the four faces gathered around the table, three boys and one girl. The girl immediately stood up, her expression lacking the judgment he had grown to expect. She gestured toward her own seat with a welcoming tilt of her head.

"Callie Foulkes," she said, extending her hand.

"Ah, no issues," Idris replied, his voice still slightly thin from the cold and the nerves. He gave her hand a brief, polite shake before moving to retrieve two chairs from a nearby empty table, one for himself and one for Amanda.

He recognized them all from the campus corridors and the library, though they had existed only as blurred faces in the background of his self-imposed exile. Now, they were in high definition. The group seemed friendly enough, particularly Callie, but his eyes couldn't help but snag on William, who was staring at him with eyes narrowed in blatant suspicion. To William, he wasn't Idris; he was a walking headline.

Amanda took her seat beside him, her presence a silent, solid support. She scanned the group, her gaze lingering on Frank, who gave her a subtle, questioning nod. The air at the table was heavy with the things unsaid, the espresso machine's hiss in the background sounding like a fuse burning down.

"Thank you for coming on such short notice," Amanda started, her voice firm, commanding the attention of the table. "I know what you've seen online. And I know what you're thinking. But before anyone says anything else, you're going to hear the story that didn't make it into the Baltis newspapers."

With a small nod, Amanda gave Idris the go-ahead. Taking a jagged breath, he began to speak. For the next twenty minutes, the only sounds at the table were the low, pained vibration of Idris's voice and the occasional sharp intake of breath from the others. He laid it all out, the brilliance of Zach, and all that had transpired. Frank was the first to break the silence. He leaned forward, his elbows on the table, looking at Idris not as a suspect, but as a teammate. The "grumbling" about his missed lecture was entirely forgotten; the gravity of the situation had shifted his priorities in an instant.

"Okay," Frank said, his voice low and steady, grounding the group. "So we aren't just dealing with a campus scandal. We're up against a shadow government that's already proven they'll kill to keep this quiet. Amanda is right. We can't let him face this alone."

Frank's eyes shifted to William, recognizing the internal conflict his friend was experiencing. For William, the idea that the system could be so thoroughly corrupted wasn't just a political theory, it was a personal affront to the institutions he trusted.

"But I'm not just going to take this on faith," William said, his voice cracking slightly with a mix of denial and stubbornness. "How can the government be manipulated like that? Aren't they supposed to be looking out for the people? My uncle is in civil service; he'd never betray anyone like that. I need to see the mechanism, Idris. If this is true... then the folks in Baltis are completely rotten. But I need proof before I believe the whole system is rigged."

"It's not just about them being rotten, Will," Peter interjected, his voice cool and analytical. Peter looked at Idris with a gaze of genuine understanding; as someone who had his own history of being an outsider and needing a fresh start, he related to the look of desperation in Idris's eyes. "It's about leverage. Idris mentioned the 'black files.' You don't need a whole government to be evil; you just need to control the people at the top of the chain. If Lumivox has proof of a scandal or a crime committed by a senator, that person doesn't represent the law anymore. They represent Lumivox."

Idris nodded slowly, the weight of Peter's words sinking in. "That's exactly how it worked. They didn't fire everyone; they just owned the people who gave the orders. Your uncle might be a good man, William, but if his boss is being blackmailed to sign a specific document, your uncle never even sees the corruption. He just sees a signed order."

Frank looked between them, his expression hardening. "Which is exactly why we can't go to the local authorities here. If they could reach into the Baltis precinct and kill a rookie like Jordan, we have to assume they're watching every official channel."

He turned his focus back to the group, his leadership taking a definitive shape. "We aren't going to the police. Not yet. We're going to be the ones who verify the data Idris has. We're going to build a wall around him, and then we're going to help Amanda burn their narrative to the ground."

The conversation shifted from a desperate confession into a calculated strategy. The air in the coffee shop, once heavy with suspicion, now buzzed with the low-voltage energy of a team finding its rhythm.

"So, how are we going to go about this?" Callie asked, her eyes darting between Frank and Idris. She looked at Idris, her expression softening into one of deep pity; she saw how small and exhausted he looked in the hoodie, and her empathy for his suffering hardened into a desire to protect him.

Frank turned to Idris, his expression turning sharp and clinical. "I'm sorry, Idris, but the first thing we do is acknowledge that your data isn't enough. It's incomplete."

He leaned in, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "If we just dump these files onto a forum, Lumivox will call them forgeries. You said you have ledgers, right? But do they explicitly say 'Bribe for Illegal Dumping'? Or do they look like standard business transactions?"

Idris hesitated, a flicker of unease crossing his face. "They list payments to companies like 'Greenline Logistics'," he admitted. "On paper, it looks like consulting fees or waste removal payments. It's coded. Without context, it just looks like business overhead."

"Exactly," Frank nodded. "That's why we can't just rely on the drive. We have to bridge the gap between the data and the dirt. We need to prove that 'Greenline Logistics' isn't a consulting firm. We need to go to the sites listed in those invoices, take soil samples, and prove that the specific chemical signature in the ground matches the hazardous inventory in your logs. That connects the money to the crime."

"He's right," Callie added, leaning in, her voice gentle as she addressed Idris. "I want to help you because nobody should have to carry this much fear alone. But to do that, we need to make this undeniable. If we can sync the exact moment of a 'consulting fee' payment with a spike in arsenic levels at a dump site, it's not just a rumor anymore, it's a legal execution."

A flicker of relief crossed Idris's face. For months, he had been a lone architect trying to guard a crumbling fortress with nothing but his own wits. Now, he had engineers.

"I suppose I can help map the legislation," William chimed in, though he still looked at Idris with a lingering shadow of doubt. He straightened his posture, his voice taking on a sharp, unyielding edge. "But let's be clear, Idris. I'm not doing this blindly. I'm going to the library to check the zoning amendments. If I find that my uncle or his colleagues actually changed the laws to help these shell companies... then I'll believe you."

William leaned further across the table, his eyes locking onto Idris's with intense seriousness. "But if I dig through those archives and find out you're lying, if I find evidence that you actually hurt those people I'm not just walking away. I will personally drag you to my car and drive you to the police precinct myself. I won't help a murderer hide behind my friends. Do we have a deal?"

Idris met his gaze without flinching, nodding solemnly. "Deal."

The table went silent for a beat, the scale of the task sinking in, until Frank voiced the question that had been haunting the edges of the conversation. "Is it not suspicious that they locked that morgue down? No one saw the body. Not even Zach's father."

Idris's head snapped up, a fragile, desperate hope igniting in his eyes. "You mean... you think he's alive?"

Frank gave Idris's shoulder a grounding squeeze, his gaze steady but cautious. "I don't want to hand you a miracle, Idris. But in a conspiracy this thick, you have to treat every 'official fact' like a lie. If they hid the body, they were hiding a secret. Whether it's his identity or the real cause of death, we have to find out what really happened in that morgue."

Idris exhaled, the hope not quite dying, but settling into a grim, cold determination. For the first time, he wasn't just running to save himself; he was hunting for Zach.

"And then," Peter said, a small, grim smile playing on his lips, "we give them the grand reveal."

The shift in the room was palpable. The fear that had paralyzed Idris for months was being replaced by a stubborn, restless energy. He wasn't just a victim anymore; he was a key player in a counter-offensive.

"So, I guess we divide and conquer," Frank said, his eyes scanning the map he was mentally drawing of the city. He looked at Idris with a finality that brooked no argument. "Idris, you're staying back. You're too recognizable right now, and you're our only link to the encrypted files. You stay back with Mrs. Potts."

"Are you serious?" Idris's voice sharpened, his back straightening. "I'm not sitting back and watching you all suffer on my account while I do nothing. This isn't a spectator sport."

"It isn't just for you," Frank countered, leaning across the table to bridge the gap between them. "It's for the families by the waste sites, the people Jordan tried to protect, and everyone else Lumivox is stepping on. We need you safe because if they get to you, the encryption keys die, and the truth dies with them. You are the vault, Idris. We are the guards."

Idris gripped the edge of the table, his knuckles white. "Well, I'm still helping. I'm not a child to be tucked away. If I'm staying back, I'm running the digital side of the field ops. I'll be your eyes on every street cam and your ears on every frequency. But don't expect me to just sit and wait."

Frank looked at Amanda, then back at the determined fire in Idris's eyes. He realized then that the "architect" was done hiding in the blueprints. He was ready to build the trap.

"Fine," Frank conceded with a reluctant nod. "You run the comms. But you don't leave the perimeter. We can't afford to lose the only man who knows how to tear this empire down."

"Okay," Frank continued, leaning over the table to finalize the assignments. "So, you get the digitizing of the documents sorted out. We need those physical files Zach found turned into a format we can weaponize. You cross-check the metadata and map the dates. Every bribe, every illegal dump, we need it on a timeline so precise that Lumivox can't claim it's a coincidence."

Idris nodded, his mind already spinning with the logic of the task. He felt a strange sense of equilibrium returning to him. For months, the data had been a curse, a weight that made him a target. Now, under Frank's direction, it was becoming a roadmap for their counter-strike.

"I'll build a localized server," Idris said, his fingers ghosting over the table as if it were a keyboard. "I'll sync the financial siphoning logs with the legislative calendar William mentioned. If a politician bought a new villa the same week they signed off on a Lumivox waste permit, I'll find the link. By the time you guys come back with the field evidence, the digital net will be closed."

Frank looked around the small circle. The grumbling about his missed lecture was a distant memory. The stakes had transformed them from a group of students into a cell of resistance.

"Let's play to our specific skill sets," Frank decided, leaning over the table. "Amanda and Peter, you two handle the morgue. Amanda, you have the press credentials and the backbone to stare down a bureaucrat, and Peter, your eye for detail will help you spot if those records have been tampered with. We need to know why that room was locked down and what the lead specialist is hiding."

He turned to Callie, giving her a nod of confidence. "Callie and I will take the field to focus on the victims and the human side of this. We got to make use of our degree and we aren't just looking for 'trash.' We're going to identify the specific chemical signatures of the waste. If the soil at a dump site matches the heavy metal byproducts of a Lumivox plant, it's a forensic fingerprint. Most people just see sludge, but we can track the pH imbalances and arsenic levels to prove exactly who 'cooked' it."

Frank gave a quick, mischievous grin. "Plus, if things get desperate, I trust the two of us to know exactly which household cleaners to mix to create a very loud, very smoky distraction."

"Please don't give William any ideas," Callie laughed, the tension finally breaking for a second. "He already thinks he's in an action movie."

"Hey!" William protested, finally smiling. "I'm just saying, if a smoke screen helps us get the camera out of the site undetected, I'm not saying no to a little 'unplanned' chemistry. Just don't blow up the coffee shop."

Callie grinned, leaning back as she looked at the determined faces around the table. "Look at us. A sports major, a journalist, and a few scientists playing spy. Move over, FBI, the Alphas of Justice have arrived."

"Will, you're our tactical scout," Frank continued, regaining focus. "You're coming with Callie and me to track the logistics. We need to know the 'rules' of their dumping, the official schedules and routes and then find exactly where they're cutting corners. We need to catch them in the act, on camera, showing how they've modified the procedures to bury their crimes."

He looked back at Idris, who was already mentally preparing his workstation. "You keep the digital lighthouse burning. If we find a name, a permit number, or a suspicious medical file, we need you to verify it against the master logs in real-time."

The plan was set. They were no longer just students; they were a coordinated unit moving to dismantle a lie that had stood for far too long.

The cool night air was a mercy; it allowed Idris to slip through the streets of Riverview like a shadow, the darkness acting as a veil against the judgment he feared was waiting behind every window. When he finally reached the porch, the silhouette waiting there wasn't a threat, but a sanctuary.

Belinda didn't ask for explanations or demand to know where he had been. The moment he stepped into the light, she moved forward and enveloped him in a warm, fierce hug, the kind that held a person together when they felt like they were shattering. It was a silent vow, a physical promise that no matter what the headlines said or how many "weird eyes" followed him, she was standing firmly in his corner.

Idris felt the last of the tension from the coffee shop meeting leave his shoulders. He was no longer just a "vault" or a "suspect"; in this house, he was someone worth protecting.

Belinda's intuition was as sharp as ever. She followed him into the room, her presence grounding the space that usually felt like a pressurized chamber of screens and code.

"So, what did y'all plan?" she asked, watching him set his laptop on the desk with a newfound sense of purpose.

"What do you mean?" Idris asked, trying to keep his voice neutral as he reached for the power button.

Belinda leaned against the doorframe, crossing her arms with a knowing smirk. "I may not be the biggest fan of the 'angel,'" she said, her nickname for Amanda dripping with its usual playful skepticism, "but I know she actually cares. And I know her. She would have cooked up something. You don't walk back into this house with that look on your face unless there's a war council behind you."

Idris paused, his fingers hovering over the keys. He looked at Belinda and realized there was no point in hiding it, not from her.

Idris sat down at his desk, the blue glow of his monitors illuminating the sharp lines of his face. He didn't look back as he spoke, his focus already shifting toward the encrypted abyss of his hard drives.

"Well, we have a plan. The others are heading to Baltis to figure out some stuff, the morgue, the sites, the paper trail," Idris explained, his voice flat but determined. "I'm supposed to get these things organized. I'm the digital backbone of this whole operation."

Belinda didn't move from the doorway. Her shadow stretched long across the floor. "I want to help," she said, her voice dropping the playful tone from before. It wasn't a request; it was an offer of battle.

"You can't," Idris snapped, his fingers pausing over the keyboard. He finally turned to look at her, his eyes filled with a protective flicker of fear. "I'm not letting you. You're putting yourself into an unnecessary mess, Belinda. This isn't just a campus rumor anymore. These people kill to keep their secrets. I can't have your name on their list too."

Belinda crossed her arms, unimpressed by his attempt to shut her out. "Idris, I've been in the 'mess' since the day you moved in. You think I'm just going to sit in the living room and knit while you dismantle a corporate empire from your bedroom? I know this town, I know people, and I know how to get information without a keyboard. You need eyes on the ground here just as much as you need them in Baltis."

The shift in Belinda's voice was profound, stripping away the playful sarcasm to reveal a core of steel and deep-seated grief. She wasn't just arguing for a seat at the table; she was reclaiming a legacy.

"It is time I stop being in the back seat," she said, her voice dropping to a low, resonant vibration. "My dad lost his life serving this village. My brother died for justice, and my mum is out there fighting to do right by this place every single day. None of them wanted me to walk that same path, they wanted me safe, tucked away, and out of the line of fire."

She stepped further into the room, the flickering light of Idris's monitors casting long, sharp shadows against the wall. "But I can't just look aside when everyone I love is fighting battles, Idris. I can't be just the pretty girl anymore. I have the same blood in my veins, and I have the same debt to pay to the truth."

Idris stared at her, the words "pretty girl" echoing in the room. He realized he had made the mistake of seeing her as a sanctuary, a soft place to land, rather than a person who had already been forged in the fires of loss. She wasn't just his landlord or his friend; she was the daughter of Riverview's own history of sacrifice.

The protective wall Idris had built around her crumbled. He saw that by trying to keep her safe, he was actually insulting the strength she had inherited from her family.

"I'm sorry," Idris whispered, his hand dropping from the keyboard. "I was trying to keep the world from taking one more thing from you. But I see now... you're already in the fight."

He turned his screen slightly so she could see the glowing interface of the encrypted database. "If you're in, then we do this right. I need someone who knows the rhythm of this village. I need someone who can spot a car that doesn't belong or a conversation that ends too quickly when you walk into a room. You're our early warning system, Belinda. You're the reason they won't see us coming."

Belinda's jaw set in a hard, determined line. The "pretty girl" was gone, replaced by a woman who was finally picking up the mantle her family had left behind.

"Tell me where to start," she said.

As Belinda stepped into the living room, the atmosphere was thick with the residue of a long, exhausting day. Her mother was slumped on the couch, her frame looking smaller than usual, the flickering light of the television casting shadows into the deep worry lines etched across her face.

She had spent the day as a human shield, fielding vitriolic phone calls from "concerned" neighbors and standing her ground against a community that was suddenly treating their home like a crime scene. Defending her decision to host a "murderer" had taken a visible toll on her spirit.

"Hey mum," Belinda said softly, sitting on the coffee table opposite her. She didn't offer a platitude or a shallow comfort; she simply placed her hands on her mother's knees, grounding her.

Her mother looked up, her eyes clouded with a mixture of fatigue and fierce maternal protection. "They're talking about eviction clauses, Bel. People who have known us for twenty years are calling me a traitor for keeping that boy under this roof."

Belinda felt a surge of that family steel rising in her chest. Seeing her mother, the woman who fought for this village every day, being bullied by the very people she served was the final spark.

"Let them talk," Belinda said, her voice low and steady. "We know who he is. And more importantly, we know what this village is capable of when it's scared. You've spent your whole life doing right by Riverview, Mum. Don't let them make you feel guilty for being the only person with a conscience left in this town."

She leaned forward, her eyes searching her mother's. "I'm going to help him. Not just as a friend, but because this is the battle Dad and Jordan would have fought. We aren't just hosting him anymore; we're standing with him."

Mrs. Potts leaned back, a dry, weary chuckle escaping her lips as she looked at her daughter. The exhaustion didn't vanish, but it was joined by a familiar, sharp-edged resolve.

"Count me in too, kiddo," she said, her mouth twisting into a wry smile. "I've spent the last twelve hours listening to people who couldn't find their own front doors without a map tell me how to run my house. But it's more than that. I've been looking at the official statements they're circulating."

She leaned forward, her voice dropping into the tone she used when she was analyzing village council corruption. "There are too many inconsistencies in this case, Belinda. And the most damning part? The 'big guns' aren't trying to find out why those gaps exist; they're looking for work-arounds to get the case sealed and the key thrown away. They aren't looking for a killer; they're looking for a finish line."

Mrs. Potts reached out and squeezed Belinda's hand, her gaze moving toward the hallway leading to Idris's room. "I didn't just invite that boy here because you asked, though I know you think I'm a soft touch. I've known Tracy since I was small. I watched that kid grow up through her letters and our visits. You don't raise a boy like Idris and end up with a murderer. You end up with someone who is too honest for a dishonest world."

Mrs. Potts stood up, her posture regaining the authoritative straightness that commanded respect at the station. She looked at Belinda with a look that was equal parts motherly concern and professional pragmatism.

"I am going to do my best from the precinct," she said firmly. "I've still got a good few on the force who are backing me there, people who remember what real service looks like. We can keep an eye on the official files and see who's accessing the Baltis database from our end."

She paused, her eyes drifting toward the closed door of Idris's room, where the faint hum of his cooling fans was the only sound. "I know you kids are doing your own stuff. You'll probably be able to get your hands on things we legally can't touch. I can't say I'm encouraging y'all to go out and break the law, but..." She trailed off, a sharp, knowing glint in her eyes. "But I know that sometimes, to find the truth, you have to look where the shadows are darkest. Just don't get caught, Belinda. I can only shield you so much if you end up on the wrong side of a pair of handcuffs."

Belinda nodded, a silent understanding passing between them. Her mother was giving them the "green light" while maintaining her professional plausible deniability.

"We'll be careful, Mum," Belinda promised.

"You'd better be," Mrs. Potts replied, already reaching for her keys. "Now, I'm heading back in for a late shift. If the Baltis precinct sends over any more 'updates' on Idris, I want to be the first one to read them."

Mrs. Potts paused at the door, her hand gripping the handle as a chilling thought finally surfaced through her professional veneer. She turned back to Belinda, her face unusually pale.

"Belinda, listen to me," she said, her voice dropping to a sharp, urgent whisper. "This story breaking on the campus site... it's brought a lot of the wrong kind of attention. Lumivox isn't just looking to win a court case anymore; they're looking to erase the problem entirely. When a cornered corporation feels the walls closing in, they don't wait for a trial."

She stepped closer, her eyes locked onto her daughter's. "You make sure Idris is safe. Stay with him. If they think they can't win the narrative, they might try to 'finish things off' themselves. And the easiest way for them to tie up a loose end like him? They'll stage it to look like a suicide, the 'guilt-ridden student' who couldn't handle the pressure. I've seen it happen in cases far smaller than this. In their eyes, a dead suspect is a silent one."

Belinda felt a cold shiver trace down her spine. The "pretty girl" image was long gone; she was now the guardian of a target. "I won't leave his side, Mum. I promise."

"Good," Mrs. Potts nodded, her jaw set. "Check the locks, keep the curtains drawn, and don't let anyone through that door you don't recognize. If the wind even breathes wrong on this porch, you call me."

The relief of seeing the local police cruiser pull away was short-lived. Belinda had watched the red and blue reflections fade, a small smile playing on her lips, her mother's influence at the precinct was clearly working. They had a plan, and for a fleeting second, the world felt manageable.

But as she reached to pull the heavy curtains shut, the smile died.

A black sedan, its paint like a bruised shadow under the streetlights, coasted to the corner. It didn't park like a neighbor coming home; it slid into position with its headlights dimmed to a ghostly amber glow before cutting out entirely. It sat there, silent, heavy, and watching.

Her breath hitched in her throat. This wasn't the clumsy, loud suspicion of the village; this was the cold, professional surveillance of Lumivox. They weren't there to make an arrest; they were there to maintain a perimeter around their target.

She stood frozen behind the fabric of the curtain, her heart hammering against her ribs. She thought back to her mother's warning: They'll stage it to look like a suicide. The sedan remained motionless. No one got out. No doors opened. It was just a pair of dark glass eyes staring at the house, waiting for a moment of weakness. Belinda slowly let the curtain fall back into place, her hands trembling. She couldn't tell Idris, not yet. He was finally in the "zone," his fingers flying across the keys as he mapped the conspiracy. If she told him now, the panic might break his focus, and that focus was the only weapon they had.

She quietly stepped back into the kitchen and gripped a heavy flashlight, her eyes fixed on the front door. She wasn't just the "pretty girl" anymore; she was the sentry.

Amanda's eyes burned from the glare of the iPad screen as she scrolled through the archived digital editions of the Baltis Gazette. The blue light felt like it was etching the details into her brain. She knew the group had a plan for tomorrow, but her reporter's soul wouldn't let her sleep.

She pulled up the two primary reports side-by-side: Jordan's car fire and the recovery of Zach's remains.

"It's too clean," she whispered to the empty room.

She began cross-referencing the official police statements. For Jordan, the rookie cop, the report cited a "mechanical failure leading to a fuel line rupture." But for Zach, the file was chillingly hollow. There was no mention of a lab accident or a specific incident, only that he had gone missing days prior. Under the cause of death, the report bypassed all standard medical terminology. It simply stated: "Remains recovered in a state of total carbonization."

Amanda noticed a tiny detail in the margins of a follow-up article. A quote from a local fire marshal mentioned that the heat at the recovery site was "atypical for the materials present." Being friends with Frank who couldn't stop sharing what he had learnt, she knew what that implied: an industrial accelerant. Someone had made sure there would be nothing left to autopsy.

Then, she tallied these reports with what Idris had told them at the coffee shop. Idris had been adamant that Zach didn't die in an accident; he had simply vanished. The "discovery" of the body had been delayed by days, giving the scene, and the body, plenty of time to be "prepared." By the time the announcement was made, the incineration was so absolute that visual identification was impossible, and the lockdown was so tight that no one, not even Zach's father, was permitted to see the remains.

The timeline was a surgical strike. Jordan had died exactly forty-eight hours after Idris and Zach accessed the "Black Files," just as the rookie cop was reportedly preparing to meet an anonymous source. The "accidents" happened precisely when the truth was about to leak.

The more she swiped, the more they looked like assassinations. She tapped a final note into her iPad: The morgue specialist who signed both certificates is Dr. Aris Thorne.

But as she looked closer at the headers of the files, she realized it wasn't just a coincidence. Jordan died on the highway, that was County jurisdiction. Zach was found near the river, that was City. They should have gone to different morgues.

She tapped on the file attachments. There, buried in the digital paperwork for both cases, was the same form: "Request for Transfer of Jurisdiction."

She realized then that Dr. Thorne was the gatekeeper. It wasn't bad luck that they both ended up with him; someone had filed specific legal motions to move the bodies to his private facility. By labeling the remains as "beyond recognition" and "completely charred," he had effectively shut down the need for toxicology or internal exams. If the group was going to prove Idris's innocence, they didn't just need the data Idris was hacking; they needed to know why a medical professional would accept a jurisdiction transfer just to bury a corporate secret.

Idris's fingers flew across the keyboard, the rhythmic clicking the only sound in the room. He was completely submerged in the logic of his code, his ears filled with the steady, white-noise hum of his cooling fans. He was blissfully unaware of the black sedan idling at the corner of the street or the way the dark glass reflected the moonlight. To him, the world was reduced to strings of green text and the frantic heartbeat of his new insurance policy.

He wasn't just organizing files anymore; he was building a Decentralized Kill Switch.

First, he uploaded the core evidence, the "Black Files," the intercepted Lumivox memos, and the chemical shipment logs onto a decentralized, immutable blockchain network. To anyone watching the traffic, it looked like static noise; without the decryption key, the data was just gigabytes of useless gibberish.

He realized that keeping the master copy on his physical hard drive was a tactical error; if they raided the house and smashed the drive, the truth would die with the hardware.

He needed a guarantee that destruction would equal exposure. He began coding a high-stakes "Heartbeat" script. He linked his local server to the cloud storage vault. As long as his computer sent a specific, encrypted pulse every sixty seconds, the files remained locked and silent.

But if the local server was abruptly disconnected, meaning a physical raid, a power cut, or a bullet through the motherboard, the heartbeat would stop. The cloud, sensing the silence, would interpret it as a "death confirmed" status.

He also added a manual override. He compiled a secure application on his mobile phone that acted as a remote detonator. If he saw them coming and couldn't wait for the sixty-second heartbeat to time out, a single tap on the screen would send a "Kill" signal to the cloud, instantly releasing the decryption keys.

The result was a digital explosive. The moment the heartbeat failed or the manual trigger was pressed, the cloud would auto-publish the decryption key. Every major news outlet, the state prosecutor's office, and a dozen secure public mirrors would receive the code to unlock the files simultaneously.
