# Alphas - The Beginning
## Chapter 4: The Paper Trail

"So, you found out that the same guy autopsied both deaths?" Frank asked, his eyes flicking to the rearview mirror as he navigated the van onto the highway leading toward Baltis.

The four of them had convened thirty minutes earlier at their usual haunt, the coffee shop with the flickering neon sign, before piling into Frank's beat-up work van. They were heading to the specific locations where the trail had gone cold.

Amanda and Peter were the "suits," getting dropped near the Baltis Medical Examiner's Office to deal with the mortuary. Frank and Callie were the "grunts," heading to the waste disposal coordinates Idris had flagged to take soil samples and casually chat up the locals about "water quality." And William had asked to be dropped at the Baltis Public Library, claiming the answer to the corporate shell game lay in the dusty microfiche of past property records.

"Yep," Amanda replied, scrolling through her iPad in the passenger seat to review her briefing notes. "Dr. Aris Thorne. But it gets worse. Jordan and Zach died in different districts. Technically, different doctors should have handled them. But I found transfer forms attached to both files. Someone specifically requested Thorne to handle them both. I'd love to walk in there and ruffle his feathers." She smiled, but it was a sharp, predatory expression.

Frank didn't smile back. His knuckles were white on the steering wheel as he merged lanes. "Do you really think it's advisable to strike directly at the source like that?" he asked, his voice low and serious.

He glanced over at her, genuinely worried. "If Thorne is on the payroll, he's not just a doctor; he's a corporate asset. The fact that they moved the bodies to him proves it. The second you press him, alarm bells go off at Lumivox HQ immediately. You won't just be asking questions; you'll be painting a target on your back. If you haven't already."

"Just ask around and ensure you don't actually see him," Frank insisted, his eyes scanning the road ahead. "Just find out if you can establish a link between him and Lumivox first. And if possible, try to get into the obituary logs or the shift rosters to see whether he was actually clocked in at the time of both deaths."

He paused, shifting gears as the van rumbled. Frank was the pragmatic one of the group. He wasn't interested in heroics; he was interested in results. He knew that putting themselves in immediate physical danger was helping nobody, especially not Idris. If they got arrested, intimidated, or hurt, the whole network would collapse.

"We need proof, not a confrontation," he added, his voice softer but firm. "Ghost in, ghost out. If Thorne sees you, he calls them. If he doesn't see you, we keep the element of surprise."

Amanda sighed, tapping her pen against the iPad case, but she nodded. She knew he was right. "Fine. No ruffling feathers. We stick to the paperwork."

The van's taillights disappeared around the corner, leaving Amanda and Peter standing on the pavement outside the Baltis Medical Examiner's Office. The building was a slab of grey concrete and tinted glass, radiating a cold, bureaucratic silence.

"Well, so how do we get in?" Amanda asked, staring up at the security cameras.

Peter shifted the strap of his backpack, grinning nervously. "Well, I have nothing," he admitted. "I'm doing Sports Science. Unless I can convince them I'm here to measure the aerobic capacity of a corpse, I don't see a way in."

He nudged her shoulder. "I thought you journalism majors were experts at this? You know, twisting the truth until it fits whatever narrative you need? Just make something up. Tell them you're writing a puff piece on 'The Top Ten Chillest Places to Work in Baltis.'"

Amanda rolled her eyes, but she couldn't suppress a smirk. "Very funny. We're not making things up, Peter. We're... controlling the focus."

She dug into her bag and pulled out her lanyard, flashing her University Student ID. "Here is the play. I'm not investigating a corporate cover-up today. Today, I'm an annoying, over-eager senior student researching statistical anomalies in accidental deaths for a Criminology thesis. I'm going to be a headache. I'm going to ask too many questions, fumble my papers, and demand to see the supervisor."

She turned to him, her expression sharpening. "That's where you come in. You're my 'bored research assistant.' Nobody pays attention to the intern who looks like he'd rather be anywhere else."

Peter adjusted his glasses, catching on. "So, while you're busy annoying the receptionist to death..."

"You drift," Amanda finished. "You lean on the counter. You look at the monitors behind her. You read the clipboards left on the desk. You don't ask questions; you just steal the answers with your eyes. I'll keep her looking at me; you look for the logs."

Peter let out a breath, the grin fading slightly into determination. "Great. The 'invisible snooper' role. I can do invisible."

"Let's go," Amanda said, hanging the ID around her neck. "And remember Frank's rule: I'm the noise, you're the eyes. We get the data, and we get out."

The van rumbled over the cracked asphalt of the industrial district, finally coming to a halt near a cluster of run-down shacks bordering the river. They had already dropped William at the library, leaving him to dig through the archives.

Frank killed the engine and turned to Callie.

"Cal, you talk with the locals," Frank said, unbuckling his seatbelt. "Play the 'concerned environmental student' card. See if they've seen trucks at odd hours."

He reached into the back of the van and pulled out his heavy portable testing kit.

"I'm going to inspect the water samples directly to identify the toxin profile," he continued, his voice serious. "We need to find a fingerprint. If we manage to match the chemical signature in this water with the residue lists from the Lumivox production logs, it would be massive. It would prove this isn't just general pollutions, it's their waste."

He paused, glancing toward the city skyline where they had left William.

"And that's where William comes in. He's at the library checking for any environmentally concerning legislations that have been passed recently. We suspect they didn't just break the law; they might have lobbied to change it. If he can find a recent bill or a zoning amendment that suddenly lowered the safety standards for this specific area, we'll know exactly who in the government held the door open for them."

The silence of the Baltis Public Library wasn't peaceful; it was suffocating. Three hours in, and William felt like his brain was being slowly fed through a paper shredder.

The table in front of him was a disaster zone. He had created a fortress of open binders, crumbling maps, and stacks of the Baltis Gazette, but there was no order to it. It was a chaotic sprawl of information that refused to align.

He rubbed his temples, his eyes stinging. He wasn't just doing this for the team. He was doing this to win an argument he was having with himself and with the memory of Idris's face back at the coffee shop.

He could still hear his own voice, sharp with disbelief, echoing in the quiet library. "My uncle is in civil service; he'd never betray anyone like that... If I find that my uncle or his colleagues actually changed the laws... then I'll believe you."

William flipped a page of the Municipal Water Safety Standards with aggressive force. He was desperate to find nothing. He wanted to find a clean record. He wanted to walk back to Mrs. Potts' house, grab Idris by the collar, and tell him that the system worked, that civil servants were good people.

He was a Political Studies student because of his Uncle Marcus. He remembered sitting on the floor of Marcus's study, watching the old councilman pore over zoning applications with a magnifying glass like they were ancient scriptures.

"The law is the only shield the poor have against the rich, Will," Marcus used to say, tapping a thick binder with his knuckles. "A billionaire can buy a lawyer, but he can't buy the truth if it's written down in black and white. If we do our jobs, if we write the rules right, we protect everyone."

"Exactly," William whispered to the empty room, scanning the Environmental Protection Acts. "The shield holds."

They were clean. The Industrial Waste Statutes? Clean.

"See?" William muttered, a desperate vindication rising in his chest. "It's clean. They didn't change the laws. Idris is wrong."

He grabbed the map to prove it once and for all. He pulled over the 1998 survey map and cross-referenced it with the 2024 version, tracing the line of Sector 7 where the laborers lived.

On the old map, it was clearly marked: "Zone R-3: Residential Low Density / Potable Water Access."

He grabbed the current 2025 Zoning Map, expecting to see the same reassuring code. But the label "R-3" was gone. It was replaced by a sterile, unfamiliar code: "TIA-Zone."

"TIA..." William whispered. "What is TIA?"

The dread started to creep in, cold and slow. He grabbed the Zoning Definitions binder. He spent twenty minutes frantically flipping through the index, bargaining with the universe. Please be a typo. Please just be a clerical error.

He finally found it, not in a law book, but buried in the footnotes of a boring, sixty-page transcript from a "Workforce Housing Sub-Committee" meeting held eleven months ago.

...Motion to reclassify informal settlements in Sector 7 under the new 'Transient Industrial Accommodation' (TIA) guidelines...

"Transient," William noted, his hand trembling slightly.

"The System works if good men work the System," Marcus's voice echoed in his memory.

"It's just a name change," William whispered, clinging to his uncle's philosophy. "It's just administration. Changing a housing code isn't a crime."

He dove back into the pile, grabbing the Public Utilities Bylaws (2023). He looked up "Transient Accommodation," sure that he would find a clause ensuring their safety. Uncle Marcus always said the safety clauses were the most important part, the "shield."

He scanned down the column to Section 14: Sanitation.

...For designated TIA zones, owing to the temporary nature of occupancy (less than 180 days), long-term Class A Potable Water filtration requirements are waived. Water sources are re-designated as Class C 'Industrial Support Channels' until permanent zoning is established.

William stopped. The text blurred before his eyes.

He sat there, waiting for the relief of finding the error, but all he felt was a hollow, cold sickness.

This wasn't a mistake. It wasn't a lazy civil servant forgetting to file a form.

It was a masterpiece.

Someone had sat in a room, using the very laws Marcus had revered, and deliberately designed this. They had taken a housing bill, linked it to a utility bylaw, and used it to erase the safety of thousands of people.

1. Book A (Housing): Reclassified the laborers from "Residents" to "Transients."

2. Book B (Utilities): Stated that "Transients" don't count for water safety.

3. Book C (Map): Downgraded the river from a "Water Source" to a "Ditch."

The memory of the coffee shop crashed into the memory of his uncle's study.

"I won't help a murderer hide behind my friends," he had told Idris.

But looking at the books, William realized the murderer wasn't the boy in the hoodie. The murderer was the book in his hands.

"The shield..." William whispered, the realization cracking his voice. "They used the shield as a sword."

The system hadn't failed. It was working perfectly. It had been weaponized by people who knew the rules just as well as Marcus did, but used them to bury the truth rather than protect it. They had legislated the people of Sector 7 out of existence so Lumivox could save money on filtration.

He slumped back in his chair, staring at the scattered books. He felt foolish. He felt like a child who had just realized his superhero was just a man in a costume.

"I'm sorry, Uncle Marcus," he murmured to the empty library, a tear of frustration pricking his eye. "They broke it. They broke it all."

His hand cramped as he frantically started transcribing the paragraph numbers into his notebook. He wasn't doing it to prove Idris wrong anymore. He was doing it out of rage.

Amanda walked into the morgue with the stride of someone who owned the place, the badge hanging conspicuously around her neck. The air inside smelled of antiseptic and floor wax, a chemical chill that made Peter shiver.

"Hey Madam," Amanda said, leaning her elbows on the high counter with a bright, disarming smile. "I am doing a small research on statistical anomalies in accidental deaths for a Criminology thesis. Would it be possible to help me?"

The receptionist, a woman with reading glasses perched on the end of her nose and the demeanor of a stone wall, barely looked up from her computer.

"We don't do student interviews without prior appointment," she droned, typing slowly. "You should go talk with Dr. Wilson in Public Relations. He's on the third floor."

Amanda didn't budge. She knew exactly what "Public Relations" meant: a polished waiting room and a press officer who would smile and give her absolutely nothing. The truth wasn't on the third floor; it was right here.

"Oh, I can't go up to PR," Amanda said quickly, waving her hand dismissively as if the idea was ridiculous. "My professor specifically told me to avoid the 'corporate spin.' I need raw data. Besides, I don't want to bother Dr. Wilson with something this small. I was actually hoping to get a quote from the top. Is the Chief, Dr. Thorne, available?"

Peter felt his stomach drop. Too fast, he thought. He jammed his finger into her ribs, hard.

The receptionist stopped typing. She looked up slowly, letting out a dry, incredulous laugh.

"Dr. Thorne?" she asked, looking at Amanda like she was crazy. "Honey, Dr. Thorne is the Chief Medical Examiner. He sits in meetings with the Mayor and argues about budgets. He hasn't touched a scalpel or walked down to the intake floor in five years. He certainly doesn't speak to students."

The air grew thick. Peter stepped in to play his role before Amanda could dig the hole deeper.

"I told her that," Peter blurted out, rolling his eyes dramatically. "She read his book on 'Post-Mortem Interval' and thinks she can just waltz in and get an autograph. I keep telling her he's just a suit now."

The receptionist softened slightly, bonding with Peter over Amanda's "ignorance."

"Exactly," she said, turning slightly to grab a generic pamphlet from the shelf behind her. "He's purely administrative. He works 9 to 5, Monday to Friday. If you want a quote on 'anomalies,' you need a floor pathologist, but they are all busy."

Now, Peter thought.

He drifted to the side, leaning casually against the pillar, his eyes darting to the monitor. He saw the name "USER: DR. A. THORNE" at the top of the active window, but before he could read the timestamp, the screen blinked and cut to a floating geometric screensaver.

"Damn it," Peter cursed under his breath. The computer had locked him out.

He frantically scanned the desk for paper trails. The receptionist was already reaching for the pamphlet. He had seconds.

His eyes landed on a wire "Outbox" tray on the far edge of the counter. Sitting on top was a pink carbon-copy slip, the kind used for third-party contractors. It was dated Nov 14th.

Peter squinted, reading the upside-down handwriting.

• Destination: Greenline Logistics - Sector 7 Facility

• Processing Officer: Dr. A. Thorne

• Net Weight: 12.4 lbs (5.6 kg)

Peter felt a jolt of electricity. Greenline Logistics. That was the name in Idris's files, the shell company used to launder the toxic waste payments.

"Here," the receptionist said, turning back around and sliding a generic brochure across the counter, blocking his view of the slip.

"Thanks!" Amanda beamed, snatching the paper. "You've been a lifesaver. Come on, intern."

She grabbed Peter by the sleeve. As soon as the automatic doors hissed shut behind them and the cold air of the street hit their faces, she spun around.

"The screen locked on me," Peter said, his voice breathless. "I saw Thorne's name, but the screensaver cut me off before I could see the times."

Amanda's face fell. "So we have nothing? Just that he uses a computer?"

"No," Peter shook his head, his eyes widening behind his glasses. "We have something better. I saw a transfer manifest in her outbox. It was a shipping order for 'Bio-Hazard Material' sent to Greenline Logistics."

Amanda gasped. "The trucking company from the ledger? The shell company?"

"The very same," Peter nodded grimly. "It proves the connection. The Medical Examiner's office is using the same fake logistics firm as the chemical plant."

Amanda frowned, trying to parse the logic. "Okay, but Thorne is the Director. Doesn't he have the authority to sign off on contracts? Maybe he just signed the check for the waste disposal guys."

"That's the thing," Peter said, his voice dropping to a whisper. "Admins sign contracts. They approve budgets. But on that slip, he didn't sign as the 'Administrator.' He signed as the 'Processing Officer.'"

He looked at her, the horror of the implication sinking in. "That means he didn't just approve the truck, Amanda. He physically handled the 'waste' himself. The Chief Medical Examiner doesn't pack bio-hazard boxes... unless he doesn't want anyone else to see what he's putting inside them."

"So he shipped the body to the dump site?" Amanda asked, horrified. "Like it was garbage?"

"That's just it, Amanda," Peter said, stopping and turning to face her. "He signed the weight declaration on the slip. It was 12.4 pounds."

Amanda stared at him. "Twelve? But... ."

"Exactly," Peter said, his scientific mind racing. "Even if a body is burned to a crisp, the skeletal structure alone weighs twenty to thirty pounds. You can't burn a human body down to twelve pounds in a standard house fire; you need a cremation furnace for that."

He looked back at the cold, grey building.

"Whatever Thorne put in that box and shipped to Greenline... it wasn't a body. It might have been medical waste, or bloody clothes, or parts. But it wasn't Zach."

"Okay, we have the paper trail," Amanda said, walking briskly toward the exit. "But paper can be forged. We need—"

"Wait," Peter said, stopping abruptly in the middle of the parking lot.

He was staring at a row of reserved parking spots marked ADMINISTRATION. Most of the cars were showroom-clean luxury sedans. But Peter's eyes were locked on the wheels of the car in the spot marked DIR. A. THORNE.

"What is it?" Amanda hissed, checking the security camera near the entrance.

"His tires," Peter whispered. "I'm a Sports Science major, Amanda. I spent an entire semester analyzing playing surfaces, turf, track, asphalt. I know what ground looks like."

He walked over to the car, crouching down. The wheel well was caked with a thick, reddish mud mixed with sharp, blueish gravel.

"This is saturated red clay," Peter said, rubbing the grit between his fingers. "It's dense, sticky, and holds water. You don't find this in a paved city like Baltis. This is marshland soil."

He then pointed to a sharp, blue shard embedded in the tire tread.

"And this? This is industrial slag. It's a waste byproduct they sometimes use to pave temporary service roads because it's cheap and sharp. It tears up running shoes and tires."

He looked up at her, his voice low and certain.

"This car belongs to a Director who supposedly works in an office all day. But his tires are covered in marsh clay and industrial waste. He hasn't been driving on a highway, Amanda. He's been off-roading in a wet, dirty zone."

Amanda stared at the tire. The connection clicked instantly.

"Red clay and industrial waste," she whispered. "That's Sector 7. The riverbank dump site."

"Exactly. He didn't just ship the box. He drove it there himself."

"Okay," Amanda said, staring at the muddy tire, trying to piece the logic together. "But I'm confused. If he signed a transfer slip to 'Greenline Logistics' inside, why did he drive there himself? Why create a paper trail for a trucking company and then do the dirty work in his own sedan?"

Peter stood up, wiping the red grit from his fingers. He didn't look confused. He looked like he was reading a manual he knew by heart.

"Because Greenline isn't a trucking company, Amanda," Peter said, his voice dropping to a flat, clinical tone. "It's a Ghost Fleet."

Amanda frowned. "A what?"

"It's a logistics term," Peter said, speaking faster now, his eyes scanning the parking lot. "You create a shell company that's registered as a hauler. It has a tax ID, insurance, invoices and everything looks legit on paper. But they don't have trucks. They don't have drivers. They just exist to generate a 'Completed Service' receipt so you can pass an audit."

He pointed back at the morgue.

"Thorne signed the slip to create the legal reality that the body was moved by a third party. That clears the hospital's liability. The body legally ceases to exist the moment he stamps that form. But for the physical reality... he couldn't trust a real driver."

He pointed down at the muddy tires.

"Think about it. The slip said 12 pounds. If a real truck driver picked up a 'body' box that light, he'd know something was wrong. He might look inside. He's a loose end. Thorne used the Ghost Fleet to handle the paperwork, but he handled the package himself. It's a classic 'Zero-Trust' supply chain. You minimize the human element to minimize the leak."

Amanda was staring at him. The wind whipped her hair across her face, but she didn't brush it away. She was too busy analyzing her friend.

"Peter," she said slowly. "You're a Sports Science major. You study ankle ligaments and VO2 max."

Peter stiffened slightly.

"How do you know what a 'Ghost Fleet' is?" she asked, her eyes narrowing. "And how do you know the specific term for a 'Zero-Trust supply chain'? That doesn't sound like Biomechanics."

The air between them went quiet. For a second, the "anxious intern" mask slipped. Peter looked older, harder. He looked at the mud on his hands, then wiped it on his jeans, avoiding her gaze.

"My dad... used to work in import-export," Peter muttered, the lie sounding thin even to his own ears. He forced a nervous chuckle. "And I watch a lot of crime documentaries. Like, a lot."

He looked up, his expression closing off.

"Does it matter? The point is, they don't trust him, Amanda. They own him. When you own the boss, you don't need to hire employees. He drove the evidence to the dump site himself."

Amanda held his gaze for a beat longer than was comfortable. She didn't believe him, not entirely. But she also knew they didn't have time to unpack Peter's sudden expertise in money laundering.

"Right," she said, letting it slide for now. "We have the link. Let's get out of here before Thorne comes out for a smoke break."

As they walked away, Amanda kept a half-step distance from him. She realized that Idris wasn't the only one at the table with secrets.

Callie wiped a smudge of grime from her cheek, her frustration mounting with every step. The "village" of Sector 7 wasn't really a village; it was a desperate collection of corrugated metal sheets, blue tarps, and scrap wood huddled against the gray, looming skyline of the chemical plants.

She had spent two hours trying to play the "lost student" card, then the "concerned volunteer" card, and finally just the "human being" card. None of it worked.

Every time she approached a group, the conversation died instantly. When she asked about the river, eyes shifted to the ground. When she mentioned the smell, that pervasive, rotten-egg stench of sulfur, mothers pulled their children closer and hurried inside.

It wasn't just rudeness. It was terror.

She sat down on a rusted guardrail, watching a group of men in gray jumpsuits walking toward the factory gates. She had only managed to glean one scrap of information, and that was by pretending to tie her shoe near a communal water tap ten minutes ago.

Two women had been filling plastic jugs. One was complaining about a cough that wouldn't go away. The other had hushed her immediately.

"Hush, Marta. If the foreman hears you complaining about the air, you lose the shift. Lumivox doesn't pay for sick days."

"Lumivox pays for nothing," the first woman had muttered, but she had lowered her voice. "We drink their poison and we take their coin."

Callie shivered, despite the humidity coming off the river. That was the lock on the door. These people weren't just living next to the factory; they were employed by it. They were trapped in a cycle where the hand feeding them was also the hand choking them. If they spoke up about the pollution, they didn't just risk safety, they risked starvation.

"Any luck?"

Callie jumped. Frank was climbing up the muddy embankment from the river, his boots caked in thick, red clay. He looked grim, holding a specialized glass vial filled with murky, greenish-brown water.

"No," Callie sighed, standing up. "It's a stone wall, Frank. They're all Lumivox laborers. The company owns them. If they talk to us, they lose their jobs, and probably these shacks too."

She looked at the vial in his hand. "Please tell me the water is more talkative than the people."

Frank held the vial up to the gray light.

"It's screaming, Cal," he said, his voice low and angry. "I just did a quick pH test strip down there. The water is almost basic enough to be bleach. And look at the sediment."

He swirled the vial. Dancing at the bottom, settling into the red clay, were tiny, glittering shards of bright blue gravel.

"I found this blue stuff everywhere," Frank said. "It's lining the riverbed. It looks like industrial slag."

Callie stared at the blue shards, a sick feeling settling in her stomach.

"They aren't just dumping runoff," Frank said, putting the vial carefully into his padded bag. "They are paving the river with it. And these people... they're drinking it."

Callie looked back at the shacks. She saw a child playing in the dirt, digging with a plastic spoon. The dirt was the same red clay Frank was standing on.

"We need to get this to a lab," Callie said, her voice trembling but determined. "William said he was checking the zoning laws. I bet my life this place isn't zoned for human habitation."

"It's not," Frank said, wiping his muddy hands on a rag. "But until we can prove what's in this bottle, Lumivox can claim it's just 'mineral water.' Let's get back to the others. I have a feeling their day has been just as long as ours."

Belinda walked briskly into the cold evening air of Riverview. She needed to be useful. She was a Potts. She was Riverview born and bred. She knew these streets better than anyone.

But as she walked down the main avenue, she realized Riverview didn't know her anymore.

The village was usually a place of porch waves and over-the-fence gossip. But today, the atmosphere was brittle. The reception wasn't just bad; it was hostile.

She saw Mr. Henderson sweeping the sidewalk in front of the hardware store. He had given Belinda her first bicycle. He had fixed her tire for free last summer.

"Mr. Henderson?" Belinda called out, forcing a polite, neighborly smile. "Hi! I was wondering if I could ask you about—"

Mr. Henderson looked up. His eyes didn't crinkle with kindness. They went flat. He deliberately turned his back to her, focused intensely on a spot of dirt on the pavement, and swept harder. The swish-swish of the broom was a dismissal.

Belinda's smile faltered. She kept walking.

She passed the diner. Through the glass, she saw a booth full of high school seniors she knew. They were laughing until one of them spotted her. The laughter cut off instantly. Heads leaned together. Fingers pointed.

She felt like she was walking through a minefield. The "Murderer" headline hadn't just branded Idris; it had branded the house he was staying in. To Riverview, Belinda wasn't a neighbor anymore; she was an accomplice.

She spotted Mrs. Gable loading groceries into her car near the post office. Mrs. Gable was the town's unofficial historian, the woman who knew everything about everyone. If anyone had seen a green truck or a strange car on the night of the fire, it would be her.

"Mrs. Gable, please," Belinda said, rushing up before the woman could close her trunk. "I'm not asking for favors. I just need to know if you saw any strange vehicles last week. Anything with a commercial logo? Maybe a green truck?"

Mrs. Gable froze. She slammed the trunk shut with a sound like a gunshot.

"You have a lot of nerve, Belinda Potts," Mrs. Gable hissed, her voice low and shaking.

"I'm just trying to find the truth," Belinda pleaded.

"The truth?" Mrs. Gable stepped closer, her face flushed with anger. "The truth is that a boy is dead, and your mother, our Chief of Police, is hiding the prime suspect in her guest bedroom. We voted for her to keep us safe. Not to turn this town into a sanctuary for killers."

"He didn't do it," Belinda said firmly, her hands balling into fists. "If you'd just listen—"

"I don't want to listen!" Mrs. Gable snapped, fumbling for her keys. "And neither does anyone else. You tell your mother that the next time she needs the town's support, she can ask her houseguest instead."

Mrs. Gable got into her car and peeled away, tires screeching slightly, leaving Belinda standing alone on the curb.

Belinda looked around. The street wasn't empty, but it felt deserted. People were crossing the road to avoid walking near her. A curtain in a window snapped shut.

She wrapped her arms around herself against the chill. She had come out looking for clues, hoping that the village's gossip network might have picked up something the police missed. But the network had shut her out.

Riverview wasn't going to talk. The silence here wasn't fearful; it was punitive. They were punishing her for breaking the unspoken rule: You side with the village, not the outsider.

Defeated, Belinda turned back toward the house. As she walked up the driveway, seeing the warm light spilling from the living room window, she realized with a sinking heart that the "wall" around Idris didn't just need to keep the bad guys out. It was going to have to keep the neighbors out, too.

Idris sat at the small mahogany desk in the corner of Mrs. Potts' guest room, the blue light of his laptop screen the only illumination in the deepening twilight. The house was quiet, too quiet.

It was a silence he knew well. It was the same stifling, cotton-wool silence that had filled his family's estate in Baltis. The silence of being "protected."

He looked out the window at the empty driveway. He knew Belinda and Mrs. Potts were doing this out of kindness. They were risking their careers, maybe even their lives, to keep him safe behind these walls. He didn't blame them.

But god, he hated it. He felt like a piece of antique furniture, valuable, fragile, and utterly useless.

"Focus," he muttered to himself, rubbing his temples. "You can't go out there, so bring the fight in here."

He turned back to the screen. If he couldn't hunt for physical clues, he would hunt for the one thing that never lied: The Money.

He had the digitized copies of the ledgers they had photographed from the warehouse, plus the public financial filings for Lumivox that he had scraped from the web. He was building a timeline, trying to overlay the company's cash flow with the town's tragedies.

He typed in a command to filter the spreadsheet: SORT BY: DATE | FILTER: > $10,000.

Rows of data cascaded down the screen. Raw material purchases, payroll, maintenance. Boring. Routine.

Then he added a second filter: VENDOR: UNKNOWN/MISC.

The list shrank.

Idris leaned in, his eyes scanning the dates. He was looking for a heartbeat, a pulse of money that spiked whenever bad things happened.

He looked at the date of the fire: November 14th.

On the official books, it was a quiet day. No major shipments. No large purchases.

But then he cross-referenced it with the "Petty Cash & Contractor" account, the slush fund.

There it was.

Nov 14th - 03:00 AM Payee: Greenline Logistics Service: Expedited Waste Removal (Emergency) Amount: $50,000

Idris frowned. Fifty thousand dollars? For waste removal?

He started typing furiously, searching for previous transactions with "Greenline Logistics."

He found a pattern.

• Aug 12th: $25,000 to Greenline. (Cross-reference: The day the local fish die-off was reported in the paper).

• Sept 30th: $30,000 to Greenline. (Cross-reference: The day a factory worker was hospitalized for "chemical burns").

• Nov 14th: $50,000 to Greenline. (The night Zach died).

"It's not a logistics company," Idris whispered, the realization chilling him. "It's a cleanup crew."

He pulled up the corporate registration for Greenline. It was a shell. No website, no reviews, just a P.O. Box in Baltis and a registered warehouse address in...

He squinted at the screen.

Address: Sector 7, Block B.

Idris sat back, his heart hammering against his ribs. He felt the walls of the room closing in again, but this time, it wasn't because he was trapped. It was because he realized just how big the cage really was.

Lumivox wasn't just paying for trucks. They were paying for silence. And on the night his best friend died, they had paid their highest price yet.

"I need to find the human behind the shell," Idris whispered. "Shell companies are just mailboxes. Who picks up the mail?"

He typed the company name into a search engine. Greenline Logistics. 0 Results. He tried the URL from the bottom of the invoice: www.greenline-logistics.net. 404 Error. Server Not Found.

"No website," Idris muttered, leaning back. "You don't want customers. You just want a domain name so your emails look official."

He looked at the invoice again. The contact email was admin@greenline-logistics.net. Even without a website, they had to buy the domain name to get that address. And every domain purchase leaves a digital footprint.

"Let's see who registered the name," Idris said.

He opened his terminal and ran a WHOIS history check, a tool that looks at the "birth certificate" of a domain before privacy shields are put up.

whois-history greenline-logistics.net

The records scrolled back through the years. 2025: Hidden. 2024: Hidden. 2023: Hidden. He stopped at the very first entry: August 12, 2022.

"Everyone makes a mistake on day one," Idris whispered.

When a domain is first purchased, the "Technical Contact" is often the person who physically set up the server. Idris scrolled down to the metadata.

Registrant Organization: Lumivox Medical - Legal Dept. Registrant IP: 204.112.55.10

Idris froze. He ran a quick "IP Geolocation" on the numbers. The map on his screen zoomed in with agonizing precision, past the city center, past the suburbs, and dropped a pin directly on the Lumivox Headquarters in the downtown business district.

"They didn't even leave the building," Idris exhaled, his heart hammering.

This wasn't an outside contractor. This wasn't a separate company. Greenline Logistics was created on a Lumivox computer, in a Lumivox office.

He realized the scale of the lie. Lumivox wasn't hiring Greenline to remove waste. Lumivox was Greenline. They were writing checks to themselves.

"It's a bribe-engine," Idris realized, his eyes darting across the ledger. "They send money to this fake company so they can take it out as 'cash' or 'service fees' to pay off people who do them favors. It's a clean way to handle dirty money."

He looked at the $50,000 payment from the night of the fire.

"They paid $50,000 to 'Greenline' that night. But since Greenline is just a Lumivox desk... where did the money actually go?"

He didn't have a name yet. But he knew that somewhere, someone had received a fifty-thousand-dollar "thank you" for making sure Zach's body was never questioned.

The printer in the corner whirred to life. Idris didn't just have a lead; he had the Corporate IP address that proved the conspiracy started at the very top.

As the paper slid out, Idris stared at the Lumivox logo on his screen. He was no longer just a fugitive; he was a witness to a multi-million dollar embezzlement and cover-up scheme.

Idris stared at the screen, his fingers hovering over the keys. He had been trying to find a "spending spike", looking for someone nearby who suddenly bought a sports car or a boat right after the $50,000 transfer.

But there was nothing. No luxury purchases, no sudden bursts of consumerism in the local Baltis records.

"They're smarter than that," Idris whispered, the frustration mounting. "They aren't buying Ferraris. They're keeping it in Black."

He realized the terrifying reality of the "Black" economy. The money wasn't being integrated back into the local shops or banks where it could be tracked by a simple purchase record.

The $50,000 hadn't been spent; it had been stowed. It was likely sitting in an offshore ledger or a physical safe, waiting to be "laundered" through much slower, more boring channels like fake consulting fees over the next five years. To find it, he'd need access to encrypted offshore accounts that were far beyond his reach.

"I can't see the spending," he muttered, rubbing his eyes. "The money is invisible once it hits Greenline. It's like it falls off a cliff."
