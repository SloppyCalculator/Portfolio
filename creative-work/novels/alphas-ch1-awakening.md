# Alphas - The Beginning
## Chapter 1: Stranger in the Village

The bus hissed to a halt at the village station, venting a cloud of exhaust that blurred the horizon. As the doors creaked open, the evening sun spilled across the pavement in long, amber streaks. It was a picturesque Friday night for Riverview; local laborers stepped off with heavy boots and tired smiles, met by the squeals of children, while tourists climbed down clutching cameras to capture the "golden hour."

Amidst the reunions and the clicking of shutters stood a figure who fit neither category. Idris kept his hoodie pulled low, the fabric a shield against the warmth of the village air. His expensive leather suitcase sat like a stone at his feet, a stark reminder of the luxury he'd left behind. He didn't look for a welcoming face; he looked at the ground, his thumb hovering nervously over his phone screen. To the villagers, he was a stranger; to himself, he was a man running out of places to hide.

Following the digital map on his phone, he turned down a narrow lane where the noise of the station faded into an unsettling quiet. The air here smelled of damp earth and woodsmoke, a far cry from the sterilized scent of air conditioning he was used to. At the end of the lane stood a modest house with a peeling fence. Idris hesitated, then pressed the bell. The door swung open almost instantly.

"Idris! You're finally here!"

Before he could speak, Belinda wrapped him in a fierce hug. He stiffened for a second, the sudden human contact feeling alien after months of isolation, before slowly relaxing. "It's good to see you, Bel," he muttered into her shoulder.

A sharp clearing of a throat broke the moment. Standing in the shadows of the hallway was a tall woman with her arms tightly crossed. Mrs. Potts didn't move toward him. She simply offered a sharp, singular nod. "Welcome to Riverview," she said. Her voice was flat, devoid of the melody Belinda's held. It wasn't an invitation; it was an acknowledgment of a burden.

"Thank you for having me, Mrs. Potts," Idris said, keeping his eyes downcast as he lugged his suitcase over the threshold.

Dinner was an exercise in endurance. The only sounds were the rhythmic clink-clink of silverware and the low hum of the refrigerator. Mrs. Potts kept her gaze fixed on her plate, her posture rigid. Every time Idris reached for the water pitcher, he felt the weight of her unspoken judgment. However, as he excused himself to go upstairs, he caught Mrs. Potts watching him, not with anger, but with a weary, sharp-eyed scrutiny, as if she were looking for the boy her son, Jordan, had once described.

Upstairs, Belinda slipped into his room. "Hey," she said softly. "Don't mind Mom. She's the Police Chief; she looks at everyone like a puzzle she has to solve. But she's doing this for a reason."

"Why are you doing this, Bel?" Idris asked. "You know what people are saying."

Belinda's eyes shimmered. "Because I know you. And because I trust my brother's heart. Jordan always said you were the best of us. If he were here, he'd have been at that bus station waiting for you himself."

The mention of Jordan made Idris's chest tighten so sharply he had to sit down. For the first time since the chaos began, the wall he'd built around himself cracked. "I felt like I was drowning back there," he managed.

"You're safe here," she said. "Sleep. We'll figure out the rest tomorrow."

Amanda stood at the heavy oak doors, handing out bulletins with a practiced smile. She had jumped at the chance to be an usher because she loved people, but lately, Sunday mornings felt like watching a movie she'd already seen a hundred times. She knew exactly which pew the Millers would claim, and she could anticipate old Mr. Gable's complaints about the organ's volume before he even reached her. The large Protestant church was always full of the same loyal village families, while the tourists skipped them entirely in favor of the tiny, "quaint" chapels down the road. But as the service began and she scanned the rows of familiar bowed heads, her eyes snagged on the very back pew. There sat a figure she didn't recognize, hunched in the corner, a splash of cold gray in a room full of Sunday best. She made a mental note to make sure to talk to this unknown guy.

Unlike the stereotypical town youth, who usually spent services slouching, this guy was fixated on the pulpit. He sang the hymns with a quiet confidence and was even jotting down notes in a small leather book.

As the final "Amen" echoed through the rafters, Amanda shifted impatiently, ready to bolt toward the back. However, being trapped in a front pew with her parents made a quick exit impossible. She had to wait through the agonizingly slow shuffle of the congregation, pinned in by her father's leisurely pace and her mother's habit of greeting everyone in the aisle. By the time she managed to break free and reach the heavy oak doors, the mysterious newcomer was already descending the stone steps. From the porch, she watched him pause to exchange a few brief, quiet words with Mrs. Potts before he turned and disappeared past the church gates, leaving Amanda with nothing but a lingering sense of unfinished curiosity.

"Mrs. Potts!" Amanda called out, catching the older woman. "I saw you speaking with that young man. Is he a visitor?"

Mrs. Potts turned, her expression softening with an unexpected look of protective affection. "That's Idris, dear. He's staying with me. The boy insisted Belinda go to a party today, but I hate the thought of him wandering the park all alone." She squeezed Amanda's arm, her eyes full of trust. "You've always had a way with people, Amanda. Would you mind finding him? I'd feel much better knowing he had a friendly face."

Amanda didn't hesitate. Amanda glanced toward the church hall. As the Youth President, she was expected to preside over the young adult meeting starting in minutes. She could see the other members, men and women her own age, gathering on the steps to discuss the upcoming charity drive and community projects. Usually, she lived for those discussions, but today, the pull of the unknown was stronger.

Spotting Frank, her vice-president, she hurried over and pressed her leather-bound agenda into his hands.

Frank blinked, adjusting his glasses. "Now? We're supposed to finalize the budget for the young professional's retreat."

"You've got the notes, and the committee trusts your judgment," she said, already backing away. "Consider this a field assignment. Mrs. Potts asked for help with a guest, and as President, I should probably be the first to welcome him."

She didn't wait for a rebuttal. She turned and headed for the park, her mind already rehearsing how she'd introduce herself.

Idris was sitting on a weathered wooden bench, the silence of the park feeling almost heavy. In the city, "silence" was always punctured by traffic. Here, it felt fragile. He had expected Mrs. Potts to give him a lecture on curfews; instead, she had watched him head for the door and simply told him where the best shade was. She was a woman of rules, but she seemed to understand that his soul needed air.

He opened his notebook, his pen hovering over the page. He was finally alone, yet he didn't feel lonely.

"You know, you're ruining your eyes writing in this light."

Idris jumped. Standing a few yards away was the woman from the church. She was leaning against a tree, a playful but observant smile on her face.

"I'm Amanda," she said, stepping closer. "And before you worry, Mrs. Potts didn't send me to arrest you. She just thought you looked like you could use a local guide who doesn't carry a badge."

In the city, Idris had always been the one to bridge the gaps, the one who ensured everyone was included while he remained on the periphery. But now, the idea of a new friendship felt dangerous. His last two friendships had ended in a wreckage that still haunted him, and he had come to view himself as a sort of contagion. To him, getting close to someone didn't just mean risking his own heart, it meant risking theirs. He stayed in the shadows not because he hated the light, but because he was terrified that his "mess" would stain anyone who stood too close to him.

Amanda sat, crossing her legs comfortably, seemingly unfazed by his rigid posture. "You're the first person I've seen actually take notes during Reverend Miller's sermons," she noted, nodding toward his book. "Usually, the only people writing in this village are the ones filling out grocery lists."

Idris looked down at the leather cover, his grip tightening. "It helps me focus," he said, his voice low. "The silence here... it's loud. I'm not used to it."

He wanted to tell her to walk away, to save herself the trouble of knowing him, but there was something in her steady gaze that made the warning die in his throat. For the first time in months, someone was looking at him without seeing a headline or a burden.

"So, what are you doing here in Riverview?" Amanda asked, her tone casual but her eyes searching. "Sightseeing, or planning to stay on for a while?"

Idris felt his breath hitch in his chest. The question was simple, but for him, the answer was a minefield. Back in the city, the "nasty stares" and the "judgmental whispers" had become a constant background noise, a toxic hum that eventually forced him out of his own home. He had come here for a clean slate, but he carried the weight of a dozen warnings. His parents and Mrs. Potts had been clear: Reveal nothing. In a place this small, a single spark of truth could ignite a community-wide outcry.

He looked at his hands, realizing he was white-knuckling his notebook. He wasn't just protecting his own reputation anymore; he was protecting the peace of the people who had taken him in.

"I'm just... taking a break," he managed to say, the lie feeling heavy on his tongue. "My family thought the change of pace would be good for me. Mrs. Potts is an old friend of the family, so she offered me a place to clear my head."

Amanda watched him, her smile softening into something more intuitive. She lived in a town where everyone knew everyone's business; she was an expert at hearing what people weren't saying.

"A change of pace," she repeated slowly. She didn't push him, but she didn't quite look convinced either. "Well, if clearing your head is the goal, you picked the right spot. Just don't let the silence get too loud. That's usually when the locals start filling in the blanks themselves."

Idris felt the tension in his shoulders drop an inch. The way Amanda warned him about the locals "filling in the blanks" didn't feel like a threat; it felt like she was looking out for him.

"Yeah, well," he said, a small, tentative smile touching his lips. "You could say I'm half-Riverview anyway. My mum grew up here before she moved away in her teens."

He leaned back slightly, the wood of the bench feeling less like a cage and more like a seat. For the first time, he allowed himself to look at the horizon instead of his shoes. Mentioning his mother felt like anchoring himself to the ground. It gave him a right to be there that had nothing to do with the "predicament" he'd left behind.

Amanda's eyebrows shot up, her eyes bright with genuine interest. "Wait,really? What was her maiden name? I guarantee you my parents, or at least the Millers, will remember her. This town has a memory like an elephant."

Idris felt a flicker of the old panic, the fear of a name leading to a search, and a search leading to a headline, but it was quickly eclipsed by the warmth of Amanda's curiosity. She wasn't digging for dirt; she was looking for a connection.

"I'll let you guess," he teased gently, surprising himself with the sudden playfulness. "But let's just say she describes this place a lot differently than the 'sterilized' city I'm used to."

"Well, you can keep your secrets for now," Amanda said with a bright, knowing smile. She had noticed the way his posture tightened at the mention of names, and she was graceful enough to let it go.

She stood up, smoothing out her dress, but she didn't walk away immediately. "I'm the Youth President here. We usually have a meeting and a bit of a get-together in the hall right after the Sunday service finishes. It's mostly just us talking about community projects or planning the next retreat, but there's always decent coffee and people who don't bite."

She began to back away toward the path, her eyes lingering on him for a second longer. "You should join us next week, Idris. No pressure, and no interrogation lamps. Just a place to sit where the silence isn't so... loud."

Idris watched her go, the space on the bench beside him suddenly feeling very cold. He didn't give her a definitive "yes," but for the first time in months, he didn't feel the immediate urge to say "no." He looked down at his notebook, then back at the trail where she had disappeared.

The "contagion" he felt he carried hadn't seemed to touch her. He wondered, briefly, if Riverview wasn't just a place to hide, but a place where he could finally stop holding his breath.
