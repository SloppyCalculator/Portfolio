# Alphas - The Beginning
## Chapter 8: The Ghost Protocol

"So, you really think we might have something here?" Peter asked, squinting against the pale morning sun as he looked at Amanda. Amanda didn't waver. She had made the call at dawn, rallying the troops with a desperation that brooked no argument. They had split the team tactically: Frank and Callie had stayed behind to act as hardware hands for Idris as he desperately tried to rebuild the servers, whilst Peter, William, Amanda, and Belinda had driven out to the city limits. "I can't be sure, Peter," Amanda admitted, keeping her voice low. "But Idris flagged this location. It's the only other warehouse besides Sector 7 within striking distance of Baltis or Riverview. There has to be something here." She paused, her gaze hardening as she looked ahead. "The alternative is walking straight into their corporate offices walking straight into the lion's mouth and that is suicide." They crept closer, using a line of dense bushes for cover. As the foliage cleared, the facility came into full view, silencing the group. Unlike the rusted, corrugated ruin they had raided in Sector 7, this building was immaculate. It was a sprawling, state-of-the-art fortress of glass and polished steel, surrounded by high-security fencing that hummed with electric intent. It didn't look like a place where things were dumped; it looked like a place where things were kept. "This trap was custom-built for him," Peter said, a grim smile touching his lips. He gestured toward the faint, rhythmic hum emanating from the reinforced walls. "And those machines inside? They aren't dormant. They're fully active." He turned to the group, his expression darkening. "Someone is in there using them right now. They realized someone accessed the nodes in Sector 7, so they set a trap here with zero encryption, a digital open door policy. But you don't leave a door open unless you're waiting behind it with a shotgun. Whoever wiped Idris... they're sitting in that building." William shifted uncomfortably in the tall grass, adjusting his glasses. The realization that they were playing against an active opponent, not just automated code, made him nervous. "If they were smart enough to set a digital bear trap, don't you think they've secured the physical perimeter just as tightly?" William hissed, keeping his voice low. "We're standing in the open, Amanda. If that operator is still watching the screens, this place is probably on high alert." "Not entirely," Belinda whispered, her eyes scanning the fence line with the sharp focus she'd inherited from her father. "Look at the cameras on the southeast corner. They're high-end, sure, but they're fixed, pointing strictly at the main gate to capture license plates. There's no sweep. If we stick to the drainage ditch along the wall, we're in a blind spot." Amanda looked at the ditch, a muddy scar running parallel to the immaculate fence, and then back at the fortress where the unseen operator sat. "Then that's our route," she decided, signaling them to move. "Since we can't hack our way in anymore, we have to do this the hard way. We need to get close enough to see who is pulling the strings." Frank sat at the heavy oak kitchen table, a fortress of textbooks and crumpled papers built up around him. When Amanda had called with the plan to raid the warehouse, Frank had been the first to opt out. It wasn't cowardice; it was calculus. He was drowning in overdue engineering assignments, and the time they had spent running around playing detective had put his scholarship on the line. He had stayed back with the explicit intention of grinding through three weeks of work in six hours. Callie was there too, sitting cross-legged on the rug nearby, sorting through a pile of backup drives they had scavenged, trying to find anything useful for Idris. But Frank hadn't written a single word in twenty minutes. Across the room, the frantic, rhythmic clacking of Idris's keyboard was tearing through his concentration. Frank sighed, finally tossing his pen onto his calculus textbook. He spun his chair around to watch Idris, who looked hollow-eyed and desperate as he tried to perform digital CPR on the server. "So... Idris," Frank started, the thought nagging at him too much to ignore. "To execute a wipe like that... doesn't the person on the other end have to be familiar with your software? I mean, intimately familiar?" Idris stopped typing. The rhythmic clack died instantly, replaced by the low, mournful hum of the cooling fans. He didn't look up, his eyes fixed on the scrolling lines of code he was desperately trying to reconstruct. "You're asking if it was an inside job," Idris said quietly. Callie stopped sorting the drives, looking up with concern. "I'm asking how a corporate security system knew exactly how to dismantle your specific architecture in milliseconds," Frank countered gently, leaning back against the table. "That doesn't feel like a standard firewall, son. That feels like a counter-move tailored just for you." Idris slowly pulled his hands away from the keyboard, a look of chilling realization crossing his face. "It wasn't an inside job, Frank. It was worse," Idris whispered, his voice trembling. "They didn't need a traitor. They just needed to be watching. The code they used... it targeted the specific encryption protocols I've been using since I started learning back in Baltis." He looked up at Frank and Callie, the devastation in his eyes absolute. "I thought I was a ghost. But they've been profiling me the entire time. They knew my signature, my habits, my coding style. They were just waiting for me to knock so they could blow the door off the hinges."

Miles away, the group descended into the shadows of the drainage ditch, the mud sucking at their boots with every step. Belinda, the girl who once rated days by the cleanliness of her shoes, didn't even flinch. She waded through the muck with a grim determination, stepping over stagnant puddles without a single glance down at her ruined outfit. No one spoke. The air in the ditch was heavy and still, but no one dared to exhale. They moved in a suffocating silence, holding their breath as the electric hum of the fence buzzed just feet above their heads. They knew the stakes; one slip, one snapped twig, and they wouldn't just be caught they would be erased. They reached the end of the ditch, where the muddy trench met the concrete foundation of the facility. Belinda flattened herself against the cold stone, signaling the others to stop. Above them, the hum of the building was no longer a sound; it was a vibration they could feel in their teeth. "There," William whispered, his voice barely audible over the drone of the massive cooling fans. He pointed a trembling finger toward a row of narrow, reinforced windows set just above ground level. "The tint is one-way, but the seal on the corner... it's degraded. If we get close, we might be able to see through the gap." Amanda nodded, wiping sweat from her forehead. "Belinda, keep eyes on the perimeter. Peter, Will, you're with me." They shimmied up the wet embankment, pressing their faces against the cold glass. At first, all they saw was darkness. But as their eyes adjusted, the interior of the "warehouse" revealed itself.

It wasn't a storage facility. It was a cathedral of data. Rows upon rows of black server towers stretched into the distance, bathed in an eerie, sterile blue light. Cables snaked across the ceiling like arteries, feeding the beast below. In the center of the room, elevated on a glass platform, was a circular command station, a "War Room" surrounded by high-definition monitors. Peter sucked in a sharp breath. "My God," he whispered. "It's not just a drop site. It's a Shadow NOC." "A what?" Amanda asked, squinting at the screens inside. "A Network Operations Center," Peter explained, his eyes wide with horror. "But off the books. This is where they do the things they can't do at corporate headquarters. Look at the screens, Amanda. That's not inventory data." Amanda had given up asking Peter how he knew things like this. There was a time for questions about Peter's sudden expertise in covert ops, but crouching in a muddy ditch outside a black-site server farm wasn't it. Instead, she followed his gaze. The massive central monitor wasn't displaying logistics or shipping routes. It was displaying a live topological map of the city. Dozens of red dots were pulsing in real-time across the grid. "It's surveillance," Peter realized, his voice dropping to a terrified hush. "They aren't just protecting data, Amanda. They're tracking people." Suddenly, the heavy steel door on the far side of the server room hissed open. A man walked in. He wasn't wearing a security uniform or a technician's jumpsuit; he was wearing an expensive, charcoal-grey suit. He walked with a casual, terrifying confidence, carrying a tablet. He stopped at the central console, typed a command, and the map on the big screen shifted instantly. It zoomed in on a specific location in the suburbs, a large, familiar residential house. Amanda's blood ran cold. She knew that roof. "That's your house, Bel," she choked out. "That's where we left Idris, Frank, and Callie." The man inside didn't sound an alarm. He didn't pick up a phone. He simply stared at the pulsing red dot for a long moment, typed a note into his tablet, and swiped to the next screen. The red dot remained there, blinking rhythmically like a heartbeat. Peter slid back down the embankment, his face pale as the map zoomed out on the screen above them. "The sedan," Peter whispered, the realization hitting him like a physical blow. "Do you remember the black sedan we saw parked outside Idris's old apartment? The one we thought was staking him out?" "Yeah," Belinda hissed from the bottom of the ditch. "The intimidation squad." "It wasn't intimidation," Peter corrected, his voice trembling. "Look at the logs, Belinda. 'Unit 4 - Node Maintenance. Sector: Potts Residence. Status: Uplink Established.'" He turned to look at them, his eyes wide with horror. "They weren't sitting outside your house to scare us. They were installing wide-area receivers on the utility poles. They were fixing the mesh network to make sure they didn't have any blind spots." Amanda looked back through the glass. On the main screen, the man tapped a group of icons labeled 'Collaborators'. A list of files opened, chats, location histories, financial transactions. "They know everything," Amanda realized, feeling a wave of nausea. "They didn't just wipe Idris. They let him run. They let him find us. They've been watching us rebuild the servers, watching us plan this raid... they probably watched us drive here." "We aren't a resistance," Belinda whispered, backing away from the window as if the glass itself was burning her. "We're a reality show. And they're just watching the season finale." "He's moving," William squeaked, pressing his face closer to the dirty glass. Inside the War Room, the man in the charcoal suit stopped swiping through the financial records. He paused, watching the network traffic monitor on his left screen. A slow, shark-like smile spread across his face. He tapped the console, opening a new window. It wasn't a map or a log this time. It was a simple, stark-white chat interface. "What is he doing?" Amanda whispered. "He's composing a message," Peter said, his breath hitching. "He's accessing the active session. He's sending a direct ping." "To who?" Belinda asked, though the knot in her stomach already told her the answer."To the active node," Peter replied, his voice barely a ghost of a sound. "To Idris." They watched, helpless, as the man's fingers moved casually over the keyboard. He didn't type a threat. He didn't type a demand. He typed a single, casual sentence, hit 'Enter', and then picked up his coffee cup, turning his chair to face the wall of monitors as if watching a favorite TV show.

The silence in the room was heavy, suffocating under the weight of Idris's realization. Frank was still leaning back against the table, his calculus textbook forgotten, staring at the back of Idris's head. Callie was frozen, a hard drive clutched in her hand. "If they know..." Frank started, his voice hushed. "If they know everything, why haven't they stopped us?"

"Because we aren't a threat yet," Idris murmured, his eyes glued to the scrolling code. "To them, we're just noise."

  PING.

The sound cut through the room like a gunshot. It wasn't the standard system alert. It was a sharp, cheerful chime that Idris hadn't heard in years. It was the default notification sound of the internal messaging system used by Lumivox corporate employees. Idris flinched, his hands flying away from the keyboard as if the keys had turned red hot. A single dialogue box popped up in the center of his screen, overriding all his open terminals. It had no sender ID. Just a black background and neon green text. Frank leaned forward, squinting at the screen. "Who is that?" Idris read the message, and the blood drained from his face so fast he looked like he might faint.

 [SYSTEM ADMIN]: Stop trying to patch the kernel, Idris. And tell Frank to log off. His idle connection to the 'Riverview University Student Portal' is clogging the bandwidth. 

Callie dropped the hard drive. It clattered onto the floor, the sound echoing in the stunned silence. Frank stumbled back from the table, looking down at his own laptop, which was sitting open among his textbooks. The screen was dimmed, but the browser was indeed open to his student dashboard, waiting for him to upload his assignment. "He... he can see my screen?" Frank stammered, looking around the room for a camera that wasn't there. "Can they see us?" "No," Idris whispered, staring at the blinking cursor. "They can't see us, Frank. They don't need to." He pointed a shaking finger at the router blinking in the corner of the room. "They're sniffing the packets. They can see every device connected to this wifi. They can see your browser history, my command lines, Callie's cloud backups... they can see the data." 

[SYSTEM ADMIN]: Submit the Calculus homework, Frank. Then go to sleep. We'll be in touch.

"Turn it off," Frank yelled, lunging for his laptop and slamming the lid shut. "Turn it all off!" "It doesn't matter!" Idris shouted back, his voice cracking. "Shutting the laptop doesn't erase the log! They know we're here because our devices are screaming our location to that tower outside! We aren't fighting a person, Frank! We are swimming in their tank!"

"He sent it," Peter confirmed, watching the screen inside the warehouse. "He's toying with them." Amanda pulled away from the window, her mind racing. The element of surprise wasn't just gone; it had never existed. "We have to leave," Amanda said, her voice shaking but decisive. "If he knows where Idris is, he knows where we are. That 'black sedan' isn't just maintenance. It's a leash." "But we can't just go back there!" Belinda hissed, gesturing wildly toward the suburbs. "We're walking back into a cage!" "We don't have a choice," Amanda snapped, grabbing Belinda's arm. "We are already in the cage, Bel. We just didn't realize the door was locked." She looked back at the man in the suit, who was now sipping his coffee, watching the dots on the map pulse. "We need to regroup," Amanda said. "We need to get to Idris before he does something stupid. If Lumivox is arrogant enough to taunt us... maybe we can use that." "Use it how?" William asked, trembling. "Arrogant people get sloppy," Amanda said, though she didn't fully believe it herself. "Let's move. Before he decides to turn our dots black too." "I'm afraid," came a smooth, baritone voice from the shadows directly behind them, "that it is already black."

The group spun around, mud splattering as they scrambled for footing. Standing on the ridge of the drainage ditch, looming over them against the pale morning sky, was the man in the charcoal suit. He wasn't holding a tablet anymore. He was adjusting his cuffs, looking down at them with a polite, terrifying smile. Flanking him were two tactical guards, their faces covered by balaclavas, heavy assault rifles trained steadily on Amanda's chest. "Please," the man said, gesturing to the muddy bank with an open hand. "Step out of the ditch. The reception is much better up here." Amanda looked at the guards, then at the man's polished shoes perched precariously on the slick rim of the embankment. She felt the weight of the moment settle on her shoulders, heavy and final. There was no way all four of them were walking away from this. She reached back, finding Belinda's hand in the darkness of the ditch. She squeezed it hard once, sharp and commanding. The signal. "We're coming up," Amanda announced, her voice steady. 

She stepped forward, shielding the others with her body. "Just... don't shoot." She dug the toe of her boot into the soft clay wall and hauled herself up. The Man in the Suit smiled, stepping back slightly to give her room, his confidence absolute. That was his mistake. As Amanda reached the crest, she didn't straighten up. Instead, she lunged. Using the momentum of her climb, she threw her entire body weight not at the armed guards, but directly at the man in the charcoal suit. "Run!" Amanda screamed, the sound tearing through the morning silence. She slammed into the man's chest. His expensive dress shoes lost traction on the dew-slicked grass. His eyes went wide with shock as he toppled backward, flailing, with Amanda driving him into the dirt. "Secure him!" one of the guards shouted, panic cracking his discipline as their boss hit the ground. Both barrels swung wildly toward the tangle of limbs, hesitating for a fraction of a second unable to shoot without risking the man who signed their paychecks. 

That fraction of a second was all Belinda needed. "Go!" Belinda roared, grabbing William by the back of his jacket and shoving him deeper into the drainage tunnel. Peter didn't need telling. He was already moving, sliding on his knees through the muck, dragging the paralyzed William into the dense, thorny undergrowth that lined the far side of the ditch. "Get them!" the Man in the Suit roared from the ground, shoving Amanda off him. "Don't let them—" A guard turned to fire, but the targets were gone, swallowed by the thick scrub and the gloom of the tunnel. He raised his rifle to pursue, but the Man in the Suit scrambled to his feet, his pristine grey jacket now smeared with black mud. "Leave them!" he spat, wiping dirt from his face. He looked down at Amanda, who was now pinned face-down in the grass by the second guard, the barrel of a rifle pressed against her spine. He smoothed his lapels, regaining his composure, though his eyes burned with cold fury. "We have the main one," he said, staring into the dark thicket where the others had vanished. "The rest are just loose ends. We'll clip them later." He looked down at Amanda. "An admirable effort, Ms. Hayes. But I'm afraid your friends aren't running toward safety. They're just running out of time."

"I AM SUCH A FOOL!" The scream tore out of Idris's throat, raw and primal. He didn't just reach for the router; he lunged at it. With a violent jerk, he ripped the power cord straight out of the wall socket, sparking a brief flash of blue light. Not satisfied, he swept his arm across the table, sending the dead plastic box skittering across the floor until it cracked against the skirting board. "Idris!" Callie gasped, shrinking back. She exchanged a frantic, terrified look with Frank. They had seen him stressed before, but this was different. This wasn't exhaustion; this was a fracture. "Why?" Frank demanded, stepping between Idris and the ruined hardware. "We're dark! The connection is severed! What is wrong with you?" "It's not about this connection!" Idris roared, slamming his fist onto the table so hard the wood groaned. He paced the small room like a caged animal, his hands clawing at his own hair. "Don't you get it? The security protocols! I was so arrogant, so desperate to be the protector!" He spun around to face them, his eyes wild and bloodshot, his chest heaving. "I routed everyone's devices through my private server to mask their IPs. I tagged Amanda's phone, Peter's tablet, even Belinda's watch... I tagged them all to my signature to keep them safe from standard tracking." He let out a choked, hysterical laugh that sounded more like a sob. "But if Lumivox has my signature... then I didn't hide them, Frank. I put a beacon on them." I didn't tell them to leave the hardware behind because I thought I could mask the signal," Idris snarled, his fingers hovering over the keys. "Going dark was supposed to be about physical movement, not digital suicide. I tagged their phones to my private server to hide their IPs, but since Lumivox already had my encryption signature, I didn't hide them, I turned every device in their pockets into a broadcasting beacon. He grabbed the edge of the table, leaning his weight on it as if his legs could no longer hold him up. "I handed Lumivox the encryption keys to find my friends. The others aren't sneaking up on that warehouse. Because of me, they are broadcasting their exact coordinates directly to the enemy. I didn't protect them... I painted a target on their backs." "I killed them," Idris choked out, his voice barely a whisper as he stared at the broken router. "I didn't just fail them. I delivered them." He slumped against the wall, sliding down until he hit the floor, burying his face in his hands. The air in the room was thick with the smell of ozone and despair. "Idris, hey. Look at me." It was Callie. She didn't shout. She dropped to her knees in front of him, ignoring the shattered plastic on the floor. She reached out, taking his trembling hands in hers and pulling them away from his face. "I can't," Idris gasped, squeezing his eyes shut. "I can't fix this. I'm the cancer. I told you." "No," Callie said firmly, her voice soft but unyielding. She placed a hand on his cheek, forcing him to look at her. "You are not a cancer. You are scared. And that is okay. But you are not going to fall apart on me. Not now." She waited until his frantic breathing slowed, her thumb brushing away a tear on his cheek. "We are still here," she whispered. "And as long as we are here, you can fight. But I need you to breathe. Just breathe." Idris took a ragged, shuddering breath, the hysteria slowly receding under her grounding touch. He nodded, once, weak but present. "Okay," Frank said from above them. He was holding his calculus textbook, looking at a diagram of fluid dynamics. He looked calm, but his mind was racing, trying to apply the problem in front of him to the disaster in the room."Now that we're breathing... let's look at the physics of this." Frank tossed the book onto the table and looked down at Idris. "You said you tagged their devices to your server," Frank said. "You created a secure flow so you could hide them. Like a closed circuit." "Yeah," Idris sniffed, wiping his nose with his sleeve. "And now Lumivox is tapping that circuit to track them." "Right," Frank nodded slowly, thinking it through. "But a circuit is just a loop, Idris. That's basic engineering. If there's a line open for them to pull data out... then the resistance is zero." Frank pointed at the laptop. "I don't know code. But I know pressure systems. And I know that if a pipe is open enough for them to siphon water out... it's open enough for us to pump a flood back in." Idris blinked. He looked from Frank's steady gaze to Callie's encouraging nod. The gears in his mind, jammed by panic just moments ago, finally clicked into place. "A bridge," Idris whispered. "It's a handshake protocol. If they are reading the tags... they have to be sending a query." "Exactly," Frank said. "So, can we answer?" Idris pushed himself off the wall. The despair was gone, replaced by a cold, sharp focus. "I can't un-tag them," Idris said, his eyes darting to Frank's laptop. "And I can't hide them. But if the channel is open..." He reached out and grabbed Frank's laptop, flipping the lid open. "Frank, give me your hotspot. I need a connection. A dirty one. One they can see." "What are you going to do?" Frank asked, already fumbling for his phone. "If they want to listen to the signal," Idris snarled, his fingers hovering over the keys, "I'm going to scream into the microphone until their ears bleed. I'm going to overload the tracking node."

"Hayes?" Amanda panted, struggling against the grip of the guard holding her arms. "How do you know my name?" The man in the charcoal suit paused, brushing a speck of mud from his lapel. He looked up at her, that polite, shark-like smile returning to his face. "Oh, Ms. Hayes," he chuckled softly. "We live in the age of information. You asked how I know? I know because I have been watching you. I've read your chats, I've tracked your movements... I've been with you every step of the way." Amanda stared at him. The smile, the tilt of the head, it was terrifyingly familiar. It triggered a memory in the back of her mind, something she had seen recently, but the adrenaline was making it impossible to place. Suddenly, the two guards who had chased the others into the scrub came sprinting back up the embankment. They weren't dragging prisoners; they were wrestling with their own equipment. "BOSS!" one of them screamed, panic cracking his voice. "The tablets! The system is crashing!" "What are you talking about?" the man snapped, his calm facade fracturing. "The tracking signal!" the guard yelled, shoving a tactical tablet into the man's chest. "It's not just three dots anymore. It's... look!" The man grabbed the tablet. On the screen, the three red dots representing Peter, William, and Belinda weren't moving. They were multiplying. First ten, then a hundred, then a thousand. The map was being buried under a tsunami of red markers, the device vibrating violently as it tried to process the impossible flood of data. "It's a phantom flood," the guard stammered. "We can't isolate the targets! The whole grid is screaming!" The man stared at the chaotic screen, his jaw tightening until a muscle feathered in his cheek. He didn't need to ask who was doing this. "Idris..." the man snarled, the name coming out as a low, knowing growl. At that moment, the sunlight hit the man's face at a sharp angle. The snarl twisted his features, peeling back the mask of smooth, corporate indifference to reveal something raw and familiar underneath. Amanda's breath caught in her throat. The familiarity slammed into her like a physical blow. She realized exactly where she had seen this face before. It wasn't in real life, but on a screen, a grainy, digitized image she had scrolled past on her tablet just days ago. It was a photo attached to an obituary. She looked at his eyes, cold and calculating. She wasn't staring at a stranger. She was staring at the best friend Idris had been mourning for years. She was staring at the ghost of Zach.

Peter risked a glance over his shoulder. Behind him, Belinda and William were visibly crumbling. Their footsteps were heavy, slapping unevenly against the hard earth. For Peter, this level of exertion was a baseline, a daily ritual of discipline. He knew how to regulate his oxygen, how to compartmentalize the burn in his legs. But for the others, this was a brand-new kind of hell. William, in particular, was trailing dangerously far behind. His face was flushed a deep, alarming crimson, and he was wheezing like a broken bellows with every step. The last time William had run with this kind of intensity, he had been chasing a cricket ball at a church camp over a year ago and the stakes then had been a plastic trophy, not an execution. They crashed through a line of heavy ferns and skidded into a shallow, dry ravine. William collapsed onto a mossy rock, clutching his chest. "Have... have we lost them?" Belinda gasped, doubling over, her hands on her knees as she stared back into the dense green gloom. Peter pressed himself against the trunk of a large mahogany tree, peering through the foliage. He waited for the crack of a twig, the shout of a command, or the whine of a bullet. Nothing. Just the distant, agitated rustle of the wind. "It seems so," Peter said, though his voice lacked any real relief. He wiped mud and sweat from his eyes with a grim expression. "For now." "I don't get it," Belinda wheezed, straightening up and brushing leaves from her hair. "They were right behind us. They had a clear line of sight." "These guys are always one step ahead," Peter muttered, his eyes narrowing as he scanned the treeline. "They don't just give up. They calculate." He squinted, focusing on a patch of movement near the top of the ridge they had just descended. Through the gaps in the trees, he could see the two tactical guards. But they weren't advancing. They were standing still, looking down at something in their hands, their tactical tablets. They were spinning in circles, arguing with each other, looking at the forest, then back at the screens, then up at the sky in frustration. "They look... confused," Peter whispered, a frown deepening on his forehead. "Confused?" William sputtered between breaths, his face still purple. "They're... hitmen... Peter. They don't get... confused." "They aren't tracking us anymore," Peter realized, watching one of the guards smack his tablet against his palm. "They're looking at their screens like the GPS just broke. Something just blinded them." Peter slid down the embankment, hitting the bottom of the ravine with a soft thud. He pulled his phone from his pocket. The screen was flickering wildly, the signal bars dancing from zero to full and back again in milliseconds. The device felt hot in his hand. "It's not luck," Peter said, showing them the chaotic screen. "It's a jammer. Or a flood. Someone is blasting so much noise into the local network that their GPS can't lock onto a specific signal." William wiped sweat from his eyes, his breathing finally slowing to a ragged rhythm. "Idris?" "It has to be," Peter nodded, a spark of hope returning to his eyes. "He figured it out. He's throwing a digital smoke bomb to cover our tracks." Belinda looked up at the ridge, then back at the dark tunnel of trees behind them. She did a quick headcount, and her face went pale. "Wait," she whispered, her voice trembling. "Where's Amanda?" The silence that followed was louder than the wind in the trees. Peter looked at the empty space where Amanda should have been standing. He looked back up at the ridge, remembering the scramble, the shove, the shout. He remembered Amanda launching herself at the man in the suit, not to escape, but to disrupt. "She was right behind me," William stammered, looking around frantically as if she might pop out from behind a fern. "She was... she yelled 'Run'. She pushed me." Peter closed his eyes for a second, the heavy realization settling in his gut like a stone. "She didn't run," Peter said quietly. "She bought the ticket." "What?" Belinda demanded, grabbing Peter's arm. "What does that mean?" "It means she traded herself," Peter said, his voice hard to keep steady. "She tackled the guy in the suit so we could get into the ravine. She's... she's with them, Bel." Belinda let out a strangled sound, half-sob, half-scream, but Peter covered her mouth with his hand, pulling her against the mahogany tree. "Don't," he hissed, his eyes pleading with her. "If you scream now, you waste what she did. Idris blinded them, and Amanda bought us time. If we get caught now, both of them did it for nothing." Belinda trembled against him, tears streaming down her muddy face, but she nodded slowly. Peter let her go. "We have to go back," William said, struggling to his feet, his legs shaking. "We have to get her." "We can't," Peter said, pushing William back down. "We have no weapons, you can barely walk, and they have a tactical team. If we go back up there, we just join her in the cell." He looked at his flickering phone, then pointed deeper into the woods, away from the suburbs, away from the warehouse. "We need to circle back to your house carefully," Peter commanded, taking charge. "We need to tell Idris that his smoke bomb worked. And we need to tell him that the enemy has Amanda." He looked at the two of them, his expression grim. "And we need to tell him that the man in the suit... the one Amanda tackled... he didn't look like a soldier. He looked like a CEO."

"Well... that is done," Idris said, his voice raspy as he forced a strained grin.

The last forty-eight hours hadn't just tired him; they had dismantled him. With a keyboard under his fingers, Idris usually felt like a titan—untouchable, omnipotent, sitting on top of the world. But this reality had brought him to his knees.

He stared at the scrolling code, watching the digital chaos he had unleashed. He knew they were technically playing defense, trying to buy time. But in his gut, it felt different. It felt like they had skipped the tactical nuance of the middlegame and were plummeting straight into a disastrous endgame—the kind where you look at the Stockfish evaluation bar and realize you are already down five points with absolutely no counterplay. "So what do we do now?" Callie asked, her voice tight with worry. "We can't use our electronic devices because we know every chat, every ping is connected to the server. We've blinded them, but we've blinded ourselves too." She looked at the dark screens of their phones, lying on the table like bricks. "We can't get in touch with the others," she whispered.
