# Alphas - The Beginning
## Chapter 5: The Twelve-Pound Lie

"So, we're going to Mrs. Potts' house now?" Frank asked, his voice tight. "Isn't that risky? After Alyssa's article, everyone and their mother knows that's where Idris is holed up. We're basically handing the police. or Lumivox. a map to all of us."

William nodded grimly. "He's right. We're linking ourselves to a fugitive. If we get caught there, we aren't just students anymore. We're accomplices."

"Well, whether we like it or not, we're already linked," Amanda said, flashing a tired, lopsided grin. "If the rest of you aren't in deep yet, I definitely am."

"Just drop a message to Belinda," Peter said, nudging Amanda's shoulder. "Ask her if it's okay to head over. We need to move."

Amanda gave a look of mock annoyance, her thumb hovering over her phone. "Do I really have to be the one?"

"Yep," Frank said firmly. "You do."

The drive to Riverview was a blur of nervous glances at the rearview mirror. They parked two blocks away, slipping through the shadows of the neighbors' backyards to avoid the streetlights. When Belinda cracked the back door open, her face was pale, her eyes darting to the empty street before ushering them in.

"Keep it down," she whispered. "Mom's in the front room, and she's out cold."

They filed into the guest room, the air thick with the hum of a cooling fan and the clinical blue glow of Idris's laptop. Idris stood up as they entered, his silhouette sharp against the light. He didn't need to ask; the mud-caked boots and the frantic, wide-eyed energy in the room told the story for them.

"You found something," Idris said. It wasn't a question.

"We found the paper trail," Peter said, stepping forward. He was holding the photo of the transfer slip like a weapon. "Thorne is moving assets through a company called Greenline Logistics. He signed off on a shipment the night of the fire, using himself as the processing officer. But when we went to the parking lot, his car was covered in red marsh clay. He didn't wait for a truck, Idris. He drove to the drop-off point himself."

Idris didn't flinch, but a grim shadow of a smile touched his lips as he tapped a key, turning his screen toward the group.

"That confirms it," Idris said. "I've been digging into the money trail from this end. I couldn't find a name on the bank accounts because they are keeping the cash 'in black', circulating it through offshore ledgers and internal transfers to keep it invisible to any standard audit. But I found the digital fingerprint of the person who built the shell."

He pointed to the registration data of the Greenline domain.

"Greenline Logistics wasn't created by a trucking firm or a local contractor. The domain was registered from a hardwired terminal inside the Lumivox Legal Department. Peter, you found the man on the ground, but I found the desk where he was hired. Thorne isn't just a 'guy' they're using; he's an integrated part of the corporate payroll. They created Greenline specifically to pay him for his 'services'."

Frank gave a slow, silent nod, leaning against the doorframe. "So we have one shark in a whole sea of predators. I don't think Thorne is the big shot, but he's the one getting his hands dirty. I found the same red clay and industrial slag at the riverbank in Sector 7. He's not just signing certificates; he's physically moving the waste, or the evidence down there himself."

"It's a closed circuit," William added, staring at the screen. "Legal creates the ghost company, Finance pays the ghost company, and the Medical Examiner uses that ghost as a shield to hide the truth in the marshes."

The room went quiet as the weight of the conspiracy settled over them. They weren't just fighting a corporation anymore; they were fighting the man who held the legal power to declare a person dead or alive on a whim.

"But there's a problem with Thorne's paperwork," Peter said, his voice dropping an octave. "The transfer slip he signed for the 'body'... he listed the weight as twelve pounds. Idris, no matter how bad the fire was, a person doesn't weigh twelve pounds."

Idris gripped the edge of the desk, his knuckles turning white as the realization hit him. "If the box was only twelve pounds," Idris whispered, "then Zach wasn't in it."

"Give me a second," William said, leaning over the laptop. "Sector 7... that name rang a bell. The rules for waste disposal changed there six months ago when the city redesignated it."

He tapped a key, pulling up a municipal zoning map. "It's a TIA now, a Transitional Industrial Area. I found the legal filings in the archives. Lumivox's lawyers pushed for it. By reclassifying the zone as a TIA, they essentially created a legal 'gray zone.' They don't have to follow the same environmental or public safety codes as the rest of the city."

"Which means?" Frank asked, his eyes fixed on the map.

"Which means they can store 'hazardous bypass' on-site without reporting it to the federal agencies," William explained. "And because it's a TIA, the people living there lose their rights to contest what's in their own backyard. It's a legal black hole. If Thorne is driving a twelve-pound box there, he's taking it to a facility that technically doesn't exist on the public record."

"And that's where the residents come in," Callie added, her voice quiet but sharp. "I didn't see them working, but I heard a conversation while I was there. People were talking about the 'shifts' and the 'deliveries.' They aren't just living in those shacks; they're the ghost workforce. They don't have hazmat suits, they're just in these thin gray jumpsuits, coughing, covered in the same blue-flecked dust Frank found in the water. They're being paid in untraceable cash to handle the very waste that's killing them. They're trapped in that TIA because the company owns the land and their livelihoods."

Idris looked from the map to the vial of toxic water. The pieces were finally locking into place. "It's a perfect, closed loop," he whispered. "The Legal department creates the Greenline shell. Finance pays that ghost $50,000. Thorne uses that money to buy silence and transport 'evidence' to the TIA where the law doesn't apply. And the only witnesses are the people Lumivox has already enslaved."

He looked at Peter, the hope in his voice finally overriding the fear.

"If the box was only twelve pounds, it wasn't Zach. It was the evidence of the fire. The melted sensors, the chemical logs, the only things that could prove the 'accident' was a failure. They didn't burn him, Peter. They moved him somewhere else inside that TIA to keep him out of sight."

"Wait," Frank said, a light bulb practically appearing over his head as he looked at Idris. "If Zach is alive, doesn't that help your case like... a lot? If Zach is alive, you aren't a murderer."

The room went silent as the weight of that simple logic hit them. The "Murderer" headline, the manhunt, the village's hatred, it was all built on a death that never actually happened.

"He's right," William breathed, the legal gears in his head shifting from defense to a counter-attack. "The prosecution's entire case is built on a body that doesn't exist. If we find Zach, the murder charge vanishes. They can't prosecute you for killing someone who is still breathing."

"So I guess the focus changes now," Frank added, the thought finally settling in. "It's about proving Idris's innocence. Once we do that, the entire web becomes clearer. We aren't just chasing a ghost; we're chasing the man who can end this nightmare."

William nodded fervently. "If we find Zach, we don't just stop the manhunt. We turn the hunters into the hunted. The moment he's found alive in a Lumivox-owned TIA, the kidnapping, false imprisonment, and corporate fraud charges will hit them like a freight train."

The atmosphere in the room shifted from defense to offense. They were no longer just kids hiding a fugitive; they were an investigative unit with a singular, high-stakes target.

"But we have to be perfect," Peter warned. "If they catch us before we get him out, they'll just move him or worse. To Lumivox, Zach isn't a person anymore; he's a fifty-thousand-dollar liability sitting in a twelve-pound lie."

Idris began packing his laptop into a frayed backpack, his movements quick and deliberate. He didn't look up, his jaw set in a hard line. He wasn't going to wait to be told he was too "fragile" to help. If they were going into the heart of the machine that had destroyed his life, he was going to be the one to dismantle it.

"We need to move," Frank said, checking the time. "Callie, you know the layout of the shacks. William, stay on the legal filings. Amanda, Peter, let's go."

"I'm coming too," Belinda said, her voice cutting through the room like a blade.

Frank turned, frowning. "Belinda, no. This isn't the village. Sector 7 is deep in the city, a maze of industrial concrete. You don't know the area, and if your mom wakes up and you're gone, or if things go sideways—"

"I don't care if I don't know every alleyway, Frank," Belinda snapped, stepping into his space. "I stayed in the city for a while, and besides Idris, I'm probably the only one who can account for what it's actually like to navigate those streets. My brother Jordan worked in the city for years, he told me enough about how these industrial sectors operate, how they shuffle people in and out like cargo. I'm not staying here while you all risk your lives for my friend. You're going to need someone who knows the rhythm of the city."

Frank opened his mouth to protest again, but Belinda didn't back down. She stood her ground with a fierce, protective energy that silenced the room.

Idris, still clutching his backpack, looked from Belinda's fire to Frank's hesitation. He realized then that Frank was right about one thing: the house needed a decoy. If that black sedan was still out there, they needed a reason to stay parked. Slowly, Idris met Frank's eyes and gave a singular, heavy, silent nod. It was a surrender, a signal that he would stay behind to be the "pilot" if it meant the ground team had the numbers and the street-smarts they needed.

Frank caught the look and sighed, the fight leaving his shoulders. "Fine. Belinda's with us."

He then placed a grounding hand on Idris's shoulder as the boy slowly let the backpack slide to the floor. "Idris, you're the most wanted man in the country. If you get caught near a city TIA, they'll make you disappear before we can even find a lawyer. Stay here. Keep the lights on so that black sedan outside thinks we're all still in this room."

"He's right," Amanda added, checking her phone. "If they see shadows moving in here, they stay parked. It gives us the only head start we're going to get."

"You stay in the cockpit, Idris," Frank said firmly. "If we get jammed up, we need you on the servers to kill the power or find us an exit."

Idris let out a long, shaky breath. The frustration was raw in his eyes, but he sat back down at the mahogany desk. "Fine. But the second you hit the perimeter, I want to hear it."

"Deal," Frank said. He turned to the group, Amanda, Peter, Callie, William, and Belinda. "Let's go. We have a twelve-pound lie to uncover."

Idris let out a long, shaky breath, the frustration raw in his eyes, but he finally sat back down at the mahogany desk. "Wait," he said, his voice dropping as he looked at his screen. "We should wait until morning. That area is their forte; going there in the dark is just us sticking our paws into the lion's den."

The group paused, looking back at him.

"If we wait until dawn," Idris continued, his fingers already beginning to fly across the keys, "it gives me the chance to map the area properly. I can look into the grid, find the blind spots in their surveillance, and calculate the risks. If you go now, you're flying blind into a trap. Give me tonight to clear the path."

Frank looked at the others, then back at the dark window where the black sedan sat waiting. He gave a slow, reluctant nod. "Fine. We move at first light. Use the time, Idris. We're going to need every advantage we can get."

Idris stayed up the entire night, the cold blue light of the monitors etching the fatigue into his features. The silence of the house was a heavy pressure, but his mind was louder, replaying the catastrophic mistakes they had made back at Baltis. He and Zach had been reckless, taking risks that he now realized hadn't just exposed them, it had alerted Lumivox, providing the corporation with a roadmap to clean up their mess and keep precise tabs on them. Even reaching out to government officials had been a fatal error; in a system this compromised, their cry for help had been nothing more than a flare gun revealing their position.

This time, the approach had to be surgical. He wasn't going to look for help; he was going to look for the Pulse. He intended to read the reality of Sector 7 through its infrastructure, away from the manipulated paper trails of the city offices.

His fingers danced across the keys as he tunneled into the municipal utility grid. His objective was simple: identify whether the warehouse was a high-functioning hub for their operations or just a dingy, hollow shell. He began cross-referencing the Electrical Blueprint of the Greenline warehouse with real-time load distribution data.

He looked for the specific signatures of activity: the heavy draw of industrial servers, high-intensity perimeter lighting, or the constant hum of pressurized ventilation. If power was flowing in high volumes, the warehouse was a place where they were holding people like Zach or running a clandestine operations center. If it was a dead zone, it was a "twelve-pound lie", a trap designed to waste their time while the real players moved elsewhere.

As the first gray light of dawn touched the window, Idris finally bypassed the last encryption layer on the Block B substation. He leaned in, his eyes scanning the consumption logs for any sign of life within the wires, determined to know exactly what kind of lion's den his friends were about to enter.

"So it is a cold site?" Frank asked, his face falling as he stared at the flatlines on Idris's screen. The disappointment was heavy in his voice; he had spent the night mentally preparing for a rescue, but the data made the warehouse look like an empty tomb.

"Well, I wouldn't say it's nothing," Idris countered, his tired eyes never leaving the monitor. He highlighted a tiny, consistent trickle of power, too small for a building's lighting, but enough for a specific purpose. "They're powering a few cameras in that area. The draw is independent of the main grid, likely running on localized battery backups or a small solar loop."

He turned to look at the group, his expression grave. "The fact that the building is electrically dead but the perimeter is still 'sighted' tells me it's a lure. Something is there, maybe the box, maybe a server but going after it while knowing those cameras are live, without knowing the depth of what we're actually walking into, is unnecessarily risky."

"Would it be possible for you to hack into those cameras?" Callie asked inquisitively, her eyes narrowing as she studied the tiny red blips on the digital map.

"Well, I'm not sure. Zach was the master at these," Idris said with a wry, bittersweet smile. "He had a way of 'talking' to hardware that I'm still trying to replicate. I'm good at working with software; he was the one who could walk through digital walls."

He tapped a few keys, bringing up the technical handshake signature of the surveillance system. "And I don't know how strong the encryption is. This isn't your standard off-the-shelf security. This is a closed-loop, high-frequency uplink."

"If I try to force my way in and fail," Idris explained, his voice dropping an octave, "I won't just be locked out. I'll trigger a silent alarm that tells Lumivox exactly which IP address is knocking on their door. It's like trying to pick a lock that's rigged with a grenade."

"But we need to know what's inside," Peter insisted. "If we don't go in, we're just sitting here waiting for that black sedan to decide it's tired of watching us."

Idris rubbed his tired eyes, looking back at the flatlines of the electricity graph. "There might be a middle ground. I can't promise a full hack of the video feed, but I might be able to 'blind' them momentarily by overloading the localized frequency they use to broadcast. It's less about picking the lock and more about throwing a handful of digital sand in their eyes."

"How long would that give us?" Frank asked, the disappointment in his face replaced by a cold, calculating focus.

"Maybe three minutes before the system resets and flags the interference," Idris said. "Three minutes to get in, find whatever is drawing that tiny bit of power, and get out before the 'Lion' realizes someone is in the den."

"Well, I feel that three minutes is a really short time for all of us to go in and come out," Frank said, his voice low as they pulled into the rusted shadows of Sector 7. The warehouse loomed ahead, a jagged silhouette of corrugated metal against the smoggy city sky.

"Amanda and I will rush in and out," Frank decided, checking his watch. "It'll be better if the rest of y'all stay back and have a look around. I hope there are no cameras outside as well, but we can't be sure."

He turned to William, his expression grim. "If for any reason we don't come out, Will, you come after us. Try to get Idris to go through with another interference. But if they manage to identify the frequency and shut it down, we're done for in that case."

"I'll be watching the signal strength," Idris's voice crackled through the earpiece from the safety of the house. "But Frank is right. If I jam the same frequency twice, their automated system will mark it as a malicious attack rather than a technical glitch. The 'Lion' won't just wake up, it'll hunt."

Amanda checked the seal on her boots and nodded at Frank. They stepped out of the car, staying low and moving toward the side entrance Belinda had pointed out. The air in the TIA was thick with the scent of ozone and stagnant water, and the silence was so heavy it felt like a physical weight.

"Starting the interference... now," Idris whispered. "Three minutes. The clock is ticking."
