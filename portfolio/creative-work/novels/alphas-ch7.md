# Alphas - The Beginning
## Chapter 7: The Honeypot

Everyone had encouraged Idris to stay home, but he had refused. He had been raised with a faith that wasn't dictated by the opinions of others; his parents had taught him that the Sunday service was non-negotiable, and the only time he had ever missed it was the morning his grandmother passed away. He walked through the heavy oak doors, ignoring the hushed whispers and the jagged, dirty looks from the neighbors who still saw him as a fugitive or a troublemaker. For Idris, Sunday was a sanctuary, a private dialogue between him and God that no corporate conspiracy could interrupt.

When he arrived at the church, he saw his friends huddled near the back of the nave. Their faces were pale, their expressions tight with a new kind of tension.

"Idris, you shouldn't be here," Callie hissed, pulling him into the shadow of a stone pillar. "If the police or Lumivox's people see you—"

"I told you," Idris whispered back, his voice calm despite the exhaustion in his eyes. "This isn't for them. It's for me."

He looked toward the front of the church, expecting to see the usual group, but his brow furrowed. The Vance family pew, usually the most prominent seat in the house, was only half-full. Marcus Vance sat there, radiating his usual calm, statesman-like authority as he greeted fellow congregants with a practiced smile. But the seat beside him, the one always occupied by his nephew was empty.

"Where's William?" Idris asked, a cold knot forming in his stomach.

"He didn't show," Frank replied, his gaze fixed on Arthur's back. "After what he saw in those files last night, he's refusing to come. I guess he lied to his family that he is ill."

Idris's heart never rested throughout the service. The rhythmic rise and fall of the hymns and the familiar scent of incense usually brought him peace, but today the air felt stifling. His unease had nothing to do with the sideways glances from the congregation or the pointed barbs hidden in the communal prayers. To Idris, the church was a body, and with William missing, the body was broken.

He had always believed that faith was a communal anchor; he wanted everyone around him to feel included and safe within these walls. William's empty seat felt like more than a missed obligation, it felt like an exclusion, a self-imposed exile born of a betrayal too heavy to carry into a holy place.

he moment the final "Amen" echoed through the rafters, Idris didn't wait for the usual post-service pleasantries. He leaned over to whisper a few quick, polite words to Mrs. Potts, ensuring that she wouldn't worry, before turning to Frank.

"I'm going to talk to William," Idris said, his voice low but unshakable.

Frank and the others exchanged a glance, a flicker of fear crossing their faces. They knew the risks of Idris being seen running through the streets in broad daylight, but when they looked at him, they saw the absolute resolve in his eyes. They recognized that this wasn't just about the mission. Without another word, they stepped aside and let him go.

Idris burst through the heavy church doors, his Sunday shoes clicking sharply against the pavement as he broke into a sprint. He didn't care about the curious stares from the lingering congregants or the potential eyes watching from the black sedan. To him, the "body" of their group was fractured, and he couldn't sit still while one of his own was drifting into the dark.

He reached William's house, his breath coming in ragged gasps. Standing before the imposing, pristine entrance, he pounded on the front door. It remained silent for what felt like an eternity, the quiet of the neighborhood pressing in on him.

"Will, open up! It's me!" Idris cried out, his voice echoing off the porch.

After another prolonged silence, the lock finally turned. The door swung open, but the person on the other side was a stranger. William was not his normal, confident self. He was broken. The entire foundation he had based his life upon had been revealed as a lie; the man he had idolized, his own flesh and blood, was a fraud.

"You're supposed to be at the service," William muttered, his voice sounding thin and distant. He didn't move to let Idris in, but he didn't close the door either.

"And so were you," said Idris, stepping into the hallway. The house, usually so warm and welcoming, now felt cold and artificial, like a stage set after the play had ended.

"What is the purpose, man?" William asked, his voice cracking as he looked at Idris. He slumped against the wall, the weight of the last twenty-four hours finally crushing his composure. "I was an absolute idiot toward you. When you were accused, when the town turned its back on you... I didn't defend you the way I should have. I trusted the system. I trusted him."

He let out a hollow, bitter laugh, shaking his head. "I spent years listening to him talk about integrity and the weight of a man's word, while he was drafting the very permits that would destroy us. I doubted you because I couldn't imagine a world where he was the villain. And now... I'm the one who feels like the criminal just for sharing his name."

Idris sat down on the floor beside him, ignoring the dust on his Sunday trousers. "You weren't an idiot, Will. You were loyal. That's a virtue, even if it was used against you. But the purpose of me being here? It's because I'm not letting you drown in guilt for his choices. You didn't sign those papers. He did."

William looked up, his eyes searching Idris's face for any sign of resentment, but found only the same steady resolve that had kept Idris going through his own exile.

"And church, what about it is real, you know?" William remarked, his eyes downcast, fixed on a loose thread in the rug. "All those political bigwigs and government officials attend it like it's nothing. They sit in the front pews, sing the hymns, and then go back to their offices to sign away our lives. It's all a hoax now."

He gestured vaguely toward the window, toward the sound of distant bells. "They are all up there still, probably shaking hands with each other, acting like everything is normal after selling off the entire village. Tell me, what is the purpose of attending church with all those fake people?"

Idris let the words settle, the silence of the hallway amplifying the weight of William's disillusionment.

"You aren't attending church for the people, man," Idris said, his voice dropping to a low, grounded tone. "If you go there looking for perfection in the pews, you're always going to come up empty-handed. You're attending for God, for your own faith, to build your relationship with Him. It's a place for the broken, because that's exactly who needs to be there. We all are."

Idris leaned his head back against the wall, looking at the ceiling. "The mistake we make is thinking that the people standing at the altar are the ones who define the faith. They don't. They're just patients in the same hospital, only some of them are too proud to admit they're sick. Your uncle's performance doesn't make God a lie; it just makes your uncle a man who's lost his way. "And everyone looks up to my uncle like he's the chief of staff. They trust him to heal this town, to protect us. But while they're looking to him for the cure, he's the one injecting the poison. Idris watched him, his expression steady even as William's voice rose. "That's where you're wrong about the hospital, Will. In this place, there is only one real Surgeon, and it isn't your uncle. "That's the irony of it," Idris said, his voice barely above a whisper. "At the end of the day, that faith is the only anchor we have to lean on. They used their 'holiness' as a weapon to make me feel like I didn't belong."

He looked at William, his expression open and honest. "But if I had let their judgment define my relationship with God, I would have been truly alone. I realized they weren't looking at me with God's eyes; they were looking at me with their own fears. If you let your uncle's betrayal turn you away from your anchor, he wins twice. He gets the land, and he gets your peace."

William sat in the heavy silence, the anger in his chest slowly being replaced by a quiet, steady clarity. He realized that Idris wasn't just talking about a building or a Sunday routine; he was talking about the only thing that couldn't be bought, sold, or encrypted by Lumivox.

"I've spent my life looking at the front pews," William whispered, his voice finally reclaiming its strength. "I thought the people standing at the altar were the ones who held the truth. But they're just the ones with the loudest voices."

Check the underside of the desk drawer, the middle one. Men like your uncle always keep a physical backup of their digital lives."
