# Alphas - The Beginning
## Chapter 2: The Headline & The Pact

"So, how was the first day back? You look like you've been staring at a periodic table for twelve hours straight," William said, sliding into the booth. He looked far too energized, his notebook already covered in scribbles from his morning Politics seminar.

Frank didn't even look up from his binder. He just tapped a particularly complex chemical equation with his pen. "Thermodynamics is a war zone, Will. My brain is officially fried."

"Don't look at me for sympathy," Callie added, already highlighting a section in her own thick engineering text. She and Frank had spent the last three years in the same labs, and they had the shared thousand-yard stare to prove it. "I've got three hours of organic chemistry prep to do before I can even think about dinner."

Peter dropped his gym bag onto the floor with a heavy thud, the smell of the damp track still clinging to his hoodie. "At least your lectures have chairs. I've been out in the drizzle doing interval testing since eight. I'd trade a lab coat for these soggy sneakers in a heartbeat."

William checked the door as the bell chimed. "Amanda's late. Usually, she's the first one here to vent about whatever local drama she's supposed to be 'investigating' for her news-gathering class."

"She was at the park yesterday," Frank said, finally closing his binder. "Following up on that new guy staying with Mrs. Potts. You know her,
she sees a closed door and treats it like a personal insult to her degree."

The bell above the door chimed again, and Amanda stepped in, shaking out a wet umbrella. She didn't look drained by the Monday rain; she had that specific look in her eye that meant she'd found a thread and was ready to pull on it.

"So, how's the new guy?" William asked as soon as Amanda sat down. He didn't even wait for her to order. As a Politics major, William was just as inquisitive as Amanda, if not more so, always looking for the "who" and "why" behind every new face in the village.

"He was nice," Amanda said, though her voice held a note of hesitation. "A bit quiet, but that's understandable. He's the new kid in a town where everyone's known each other since the crib."

"Well, your quiet friend seems to be a bit of a whiz," William countered, leaning in. "Tim told me he's already enrolled in the Computer Science program at the college. Apparently, he transferred in from Baltis Institute of Technology. Tim said he basically outperformed the entire class on the first day."

Frank looked up, his interest finally piqued. "Wait, he came from Baltis Tech? That's a top-tier research university. If he was a senior there, transferring here isn't a lateral move; it's a massive step down."

"That explains the paperwork," Frank continued, tapping his chin thoughtfully. "Usually, transferring into the final year mid-semester is impossible because of credit matching. But if he's coming from a place like that, their 'Intro to Systems' is probably harder than our entire final thesis. The Dean didn't bend the rules, Will; she probably just looked at his transcript and realized he's already done our curriculum twice over."

The table went quiet for a beat. The news changed the narrative. Idris wasn't just a stray guest of Mrs. Potts anymore; he was a highly capable anomaly.

"That's the thing," Amanda said softly, stirring her sugar into her coffee. "He told me he's half-Riverview. His mum grew up here. But when I asked about names, he shut down. He's scared of something, Will. I don't think he's here because he wants a degree; I think he's here because he's overqualified for the life he's currently allowed to have."

"He didn't just transfer," William added, tapping his fingers on the table. "He was fast-tracked. He's sitting in advanced labs for units we've been prepping for since last spring, and according to Tim, he's acting like it's introductory math. He's not learning to type, Frank; he's just killing time until graduation. You don't drop from an elite institute to a village college unless you have some serious pull, or a serious need to disappear."

"Or," Peter added, looking from Amanda to William, "he's here because nobody in the city would let him stay. If he's a genius who suddenly needs to be 'invisible' in a village, that's not just a change of pace. That's a relocation."

The table quieted down, the hum of the coffee shop feeling suddenly distant.

"Well, I don't think his secret can last long here," Frank said with a light laugh. As the smartest one in the group, the idea of another heavy-hitter in the science department, someone who had survived the rigors of Baltis Tech, intrigued him. He wasn't threatened; he was curious to see if the rumors were true

"Yeah, true," Peter said, leaning back. His voice was quieter, more serious than usual. "But I think it's better to stay quiet. We don't actually know what he's going through."

Being the only one at the table not originally from Riverview, Peter understood the weight of a fresh start. He had his own history to contend with, secrets he'd had to pack away tightly before he ever set foot in the village. He knew that sometimes, a new life wasn't a choice; it was a necessity.

"Yeah, Peter's right," Frank agreed, catching the weight in Peter's tone. He turned his gaze toward the head of the table. "Let the guy settle rather than treating him like a new mystery to break."

He said it pointedly, looking at Amanda. She didn't respond immediately. She was looking out the window, distracted, her mind already spinning with a million different thoughts and headlines. For her, Idris wasn't just a student, he was a puzzle that felt like it was missing its most important piece.

Amanda barely heard the clink of Frank's cup hitting the saucer. She was thinking about the way Idris had looked in the park, not like a "whiz" or a "prodigy," but like someone who was waiting for a blow to land.

"I'm not trying to 'break' him, Frank," Amanda said finally, pulling her gaze away from the window. "I just... I want him to feel like he doesn't have to look over his shoulder every five minutes. If he's going to settle in, he needs someone who isn't going to treat him like a stranger."

"But you're still going to try and find out who his mother was, aren't you?" William asked, a playful but knowing smirk on his face.

Amanda sighed, gathering her things. "Maybe. But only because if I can find a connection, it might make him feel less like an outsider. There's a difference between being nosy and being a neighbor."

"Just be careful, Amanda," Peter warned as they all stood up to face the Monday rain. "Sometimes when you start pulling at a loose thread, the whole thing uncurls faster than you can fix it."

As they walked out of the shop, the group split up toward their respective dorms and houses. Amanda, however, found herself walking the long way past the Computer Science building. She told herself she was just clearing her head after a long day of Journalism ethics, but as she looked up at the glowing lights of the advanced labs, she knew she was already looking for the "slate-gray" silhouette of the boy who didn't want to be found.

On Tuesday afternoon, the atmosphere at Riverview Community College shifted from the quiet buzz of the morning to the frantic energy of the day's midpoint. While the "Core Five" were scattered across their respective seminars, Idris found himself in the one place he felt both most capable and most exposed: the Advanced Systems Lab.

The room was filled with the rhythmic clicking of mechanical keyboards and the low hum of cooling fans. Idris sat in the far corner, his screen a waterfall of code that would have made most seniors dizzy. He was working through a complex data structure assignment that was supposed to take a week; he was nearly finished in forty minutes.

Across the room, Tim, the student William had mentioned, kept glancing over, his jaw practically dropping every time he saw Idris's cursor fly.

Just as Idris was about to compile his work, the heavy lab door swung open. Amanda walked in, holding a stack of freshly printed proofs for the newspaper. She caught Idris's eye and offered a small, hesitant wave.

Before she could walk over, Alyssa entered right behind her, complaining loudly about a dead lead.

"I'm telling you, Amanda, the school board budget is a dead end. We need something with more punch," Alyssa said, her voice cutting through the lab's quiet.

Idris immediately stiffened. He didn't know Alyssa, but he recognized that tone, the sharp, predatory edge of someone looking for a story. He instinctively hunched his shoulders, pulling his hoodie a little tighter, and hit a key to clear his screen of the advanced work. To anyone passing by, he wanted to look like just another student struggling with a basic loop.

Amanda noticed the change in him instantly. She saw the "whiz" vanish and the "stranger running out of places to hide" return.

"I'll be out in a second, Alyssa," Amanda said, trying to steer her co-editor back toward the hallway. "I just need to check a fact with one of the CS students."

Alyssa glanced toward the back corner, her eyes sweeping over Idris with total boredom. "Don't take too long. You're wasting time on the small stuff again."

As the door clicked shut behind Alyssa, Amanda walked toward Idris's terminal. The silence between them was different today; it was charged with the knowledge that people were starting to notice him.

"She's gone," Amanda said softly, leaning against the edge of his desk. "And for the record, she thinks you're boring. Which, in this town, is actually a huge compliment."

Idris finally looked up, his grip on his mouse relaxing just a fraction. "Is that what people think? That I'm boring?"

"Only the ones who aren't paying attention," Amanda replied, her gaze dropping to his darkened screen. "But word travels fast. People are talking about how you're 'fast-tracked.' You're making it hard for me to keep your secrets, Idris."

Idris looked at the darkened monitor, his own reflection staring back at him with hollow eyes. He stayed silent for a long moment, the only sound between them being the distant, rhythmic tap of other students' keyboards.

"Amanda," he said, his voice barely a whisper, yet carrying a weight that made her breath hitch. He finally looked up at her, and for the first time, the guarded walls in his eyes flickered with something like genuine fear. "I appreciate what you're doing. Really. But some threads... they aren't meant to be pulled. The more you look for a connection, the more you put yourself, and your friends, in the path of things you aren't ready for. Please. Just let me be 'the boring guy' staying with the Police Chief."

It wasn't a threat; it was a plea. He wasn't warning her away to protect his ego, but to protect her peace.

Amanda opened her mouth to argue, to tell him that Riverview was safe, but the look in his eyes stopped her. It was the look of someone who had seen exactly how fast a life could unspool. Before she could find the right words, the lab door swung open again.

"Amanda! The meeting is starting in two minutes. The Principal is already breathing down the staff's necks about the budget editorial!" Alyssa's voice boomed from the doorway.

Amanda straightened up, her heart heavy with Idris's warning. She gave him a final, lingering look, one that promised she had heard him, even if she wasn't sure she could obey, and turned to follow Alyssa.

The late afternoon sun cast long, skeletal shadows across the computer lab. Most of the staff had already headed home, leaving only Amanda and Alyssa tethered to their desks by the looming print deadline. Alyssa was a blur of efficiency, but Amanda's screen remained frozen on a blank search bar.

Idris's warning, some threads aren't meant to be pulled, felt like a physical weight in the room. But the journalist in her was louder than the fear. She opened a fresh browser tab and typed the only two anchors she had: Idris. Baltis.

She clicked the "News" tab.

As the results loaded, Amanda's breath left her in a sharp gasp. She didn't stay to read the articles. The lead image, a cold, high-resolution police file photo of Idris, was enough to turn her blood to ice.

She stood up so abruptly her chair screeched against the floor. Panic, cold and sharp, surged through her. She thought of Mrs. Potts, of Belinda, and of the seat he had taken in her church.

"I—I have to go," she stammered, her voice thin as she scrambled to grab her bag.

Alyssa stopped typing, her eyes flicking up. "What? Amanda, we have the print deadline. You can't just walk out."

"I'm sick, Alyssa. I just... I need to go." Amanda didn't wait for a rebuttal. She bolted from the room, her mind a whirlwind of terror. In her frantic rush, she instinctively hit a key that minimized the window, leaving the desktop looking innocent and empty.

Alyssa watched the door swing shut, her expression shifting from annoyance to sharp suspicion. Amanda didn't just walk out on deadlines.

Sensing a secret, Alyssa slid her chair over to Amanda's terminal. The screen was blank, but Alyssa knew Amanda too well. She clicked the browser menu and pulled up the History. Her eyes scanned the most recent entries, landing on the repeated search: Idris Baltis News.

She clicked the top link.

As the page restored, a slow, predatory smile spread across Alyssa's face. The headline was bold, black, and final: "IDRIS DEVLIN: PRIMARY SUSPECT IN BALTIS CAMPUS MURDER."

Alyssa's eyes gleamed with a dark, triumphant joy. She wasn't seeing a tragedy; she was seeing a career-making front page.

"Well, Idris Devlin," Alyssa whispered to the empty room, her fingers already reaching for the 'Print Screen' key. "You aren't so boring after all."

Wednesday arrived under a sky the color of a healing bruise. Idris moved through the campus gates with his hoodie pulled low, navigating the morning rush with the practiced invisibility of a ghost. For weeks, this camouflage had been his lifeline. The Dean, a woman who guarded his transfer file like a state secret had bought him time, and the students had largely ignored him, dismissing him as just another quiet transfer in the back of the lab other than the students awed by his performances at college. But the moment the heavy glass doors of the main hall swung shut behind him, the atmosphere shifted. It wasn't a gradual change. It was instant, like a circuit breaker tripping. The ambient hum of morning chatter didn't just fade; it was sucked out of the room, replaced by a silence so heavy it pressed against his eardrums. He felt the eyes before he saw them, hundreds of them, prickly and cold, tracking his movement across the tile floor. It was a sensation he remembered too well from Baltis: the feeling of being a contagion. He stopped. The air in the hall tasted metallic. There, abandoned on a central table like a piece of evidence, lay the source of the sudden frost. The student newspaper was splayed open, the ink stark and black against the cheap paper. The words weren't just a headline; they were a verdict.

IDRIS DEVLIN: A KILLER IN OUR MIDST

The floor seemed to tilt beneath him. The fragile sanctuary he had spent months constructing the quiet guest room, the anonymity, the hope of a fresh start didn't just crack. It evaporated. To the Dean, he was a favor; to the sea of faces now recoiling from him, he was exactly what the paper said: a monster who had tricked his way into the fold. "Idris!" The voice cut through the static in his head. Amanda came sprinting through the parted crowd, her face stripped of color, her eyes wide with a frantic, terrified guilt. She reached him just as he tore his gaze away from his own name. "Idris, I am so sorry... please, God, this wasn't meant to happen," she stammered, breathless. She reached out, her fingers grazing his sleeve. Ideally, he would have pulled away. Instead, he flinched as if her hand were a branding iron. The contact snapped the last tether of his control. The months of looking over his shoulder, the exhaustion of carrying a dead friend's memory, and the sheer physical effort of pretending to be human, it all collapsed into a white-hot singularity of betrayal. She was the Editor. In his mind, she hadn't just made a mistake; she had hunted him. "What wasn't meant to happen?" he shouted. The raw sound ripped out of his throat, echoing off the high vaulted ceiling and freezing the few remaining whispers. "That you got your story? Or that I found out?" "Idris, no, I—" "I told you!" His voice cracked, wavering between rage and agonizing grief. "I told you to leave me alone! I begged you to let me be invisible!" He looked at her, really looked at her, and saw the fear in her eyes. But he didn't see a friend anymore. He saw the mechanism that had destroyed his life. "Why couldn't you just let me exist?" he whispered, the question landing in the silence like a stone. The crowd stepped back, unsettled by the naked agony in his tone. Idris didn't wait for a defense. He couldn't breathe in this air anymore. With a choked sound that was half-sob, half-growl, he turned and bolted, shouldering through the heavy doors and vanishing into the gray, unforgiving morning.

Amanda stood frozen, her hand still reaching out into the empty air where Idris had been. The echoes of his shouting seemed to vibrate in the silence that followed. Every eye in the hall was on her, some with pity, but most with the sharp, cold judgment of people who felt they had been lied to.

"Amanda? What the hell is this?"

She turned to see William and Frank standing by the entrance, staring at the newspaper on the table. William looked horrified; as a Politics major, he was ashamed with the fact of being friends with a girl standing in the way of justice. Frank, usually the logical one, looked at the headline and then back at Amanda, his disappointment evident.

"You knew?" Frank asked, his voice low. "You've been distracted for days because of him. You knew who he was and you didn't say a word to us?"

"It's not what it looks like!" Amanda cried, her voice breaking. "I was trying to help him settle in, I didn't—"

"Help him settle in?" William snapped, gesturing to the bold black ink on the table. "Amanda, you were trying to bring a murder suspect into our group. You almost brought that mess into our lives without a warning."

Before she could defend herself, a slow, rhythmic clapping sounded from the stairs. Alyssa descended, a smirk of pure triumph on her face. She looked like she had just won an election.

"Don't be so hard on her, boys," Alyssa said, her voice dripping with fake sympathy. "Our Editor-in-Chief just didn't have the stomach for the truth. I simply did the job she was too 'emotional' to finish."

Alyssa walked straight up to Amanda, leaning in close so only she could hear. "You lost control of your paper, and you lost your little project. Next time, remember: a journalist doesn't have friends. We only have subjects."

Frank felt a sharp pang of sympathy as he watched Amanda tremble. While William was already attempting to building a political defense, Frank looked at his friend and saw only a girl who had tried to do a good thing that had gone horribly wrong. He realized she had meant well; she hadn't been hiding a monster, she had been trying to shield a person.

He saw her eyes dart toward the door, filled with a frantic need to follow Idris, yet frozen by the weight of the crowd's uproar and the harshness of William's words.

Unlike William, Frank didn't like jumping to conclusions. He lived in the world of evidence and controlled variables, and he knew Amanda better than anyone. Reaching out, he firmly gripped William's arm, a silent instruction for his friend to back down and stay calm.

When Amanda looked at him, her eyes pleading and confused, Frank gave a small, steadying nod. With a slight, almost imperceptible gesture of his head toward the exit, he offered her the silent encouragement she needed.

Go, the gesture said. I'll handle things here.

Amanda gave a quick, grateful nod to Frank and bolted through the doors, her lungs burning in the cold morning air. She didn't stop until she reached the gate of Mrs. Potts' house. Her mind was a mess of Idris's angry face and the cold print of the newspaper, but one thing was certain: she had to find him.

She hammered on the door, the sound echoing through the quiet street. When the latch finally clicked, Amanda prepared herself for Mrs. Potts, but to her dismay, it was Belinda who stood there.

"What are you doing here, troublemaker?" Belinda's voice was harsh, cutting through Amanda's frantic energy like a knife.

The two had never been friends. To Belinda, Amanda was a "prim show-off" who acted like she owned every room she walked into. To Amanda, Belinda was vain, selfish, and far too focused on appearances. But as Amanda looked at her now, the usual sting of their rivalry felt small. The selfish glint in Belinda's eyes was gone, replaced by deep, jagged lines of worry.

"Is he here?" Amanda gasped, ignoring the insult. "Belinda, please. I need to explain."

Belinda didn't move an inch to let her in. She gripped the edge of the door, her knuckles white. "You've done enough explaining for one day, haven't you? The whole village is buzzing because of your little paper.

The worry on Belinda's face confirmed Amanda's worst fear: the news hadn't just reached the college; it had hit Idris like a physical blow.

"I didn't write it!" Amanda cried, her voice cracking. "It was Alyssa. She went behind my back. I was trying to protect him."

"Is he upstairs?" Amanda asked, trying to peer past her into the hallway. "I have to tell him I didn't do this."

"He's not here, Amanda," Belinda said, her voice dropping to a whisper.

Amanda froze. "What do you mean he's not here? Where did he go?"

"He came back from the college like a ghost," Belinda explained, her hands trembling slightly as she tucked a stray hair behind her ear. "He didn't even say a word. He just grabbed his jacket and went out the back door before my mother could stop him. She's out looking for him now, driving the patrol car toward the old station."

Belinda looked out at the street, her eyes searching the gray, bruised horizon. The vanity Amanda usually saw in her was completely gone, replaced by a raw, protective fear. "He's terrified, Amanda. He isn't just scared of the neighbors; he's terrified of the corporation finding out he's here. He thinks this town just turned into a cage."

What corporation? Amanda wanted to ask, the word ringing in her head like a warning bell. Her journalism instincts flared, but she held back. This wasn't the time for questions or leads; this was a rescue mission.

Amanda felt a cold pit form in her stomach. If Idris wasn't at the house, he was vulnerable. In a small village where everyone now thought he was a killer, and with a mysterious corporation potentially tracking him. there was nowhere for him to go. Except, perhaps, the places he felt a connection to.

"The park," Amanda whispered, more to herself than to Belinda. "The bench where we sat on Sunday. Or the old ruins by the creek."

Belinda finally looked Amanda in the eye, their lifelong rivalry momentarily forgotten in the face of a shared crisis. "If you find him first, don't scream at him. And for heaven's sake, don't bring a camera or a notebook. Just... bring him back before someone in this village decides to play hero."

The realization hit Amanda like a physical blow. She didn't head for the park or the ruins. She remembered the way he had watched the doors on Sunday, eyeing the thickness of the stone and the height of the rafters. He wasn't looking for a place to be forgiven, he was looking for a safe space. A place where the noise of the world and the reach of the digital grid felt a hundred miles away.

She ran toward the stone archways of the village church, her shoes clicking loudly against the cobblestones. The heavy oak doors were unlocked, and as she pushed them open, the cool, incense-heavy air of the nave rushed to meet her. Without surprise, she saw him. He was kneeling at the very last pew, his forehead resting against the cool wood of the seat in front of him. He wasn't praying; he was just holding on.

His shoulders were hunched, shaking with the force of silent, jagged sobs. Tears streamed down his face, dripping onto the floor, but he made no sound. He looked like someone who had been running for a thousand miles and had finally found the only room left with a lock on the door. Amanda stepped softly, the grit of the stone floor crunching under her feet. He didn't turn around. He didn't have to.

"Idris," she whispered, her voice barely audible.

He didn't look up at first. "I told you," he choked out, his voice wrecked and raw. "I told you what would happen if people started looking. I just needed a place where I could breathe for a second. Somewhere they couldn't see me. Why couldn't you just let me have that?"

He finally lifted his head, his eyes red-rimmed and hollow. The raw fury that had exploded in the university hall was gone, replaced by a crushing exhaustion. Seeing Amanda standing there, breathless, pale, and trembling with genuine worry, the sharpness in his gaze softened. He looked at her apologetically, the weight of his earlier outburst at the hall clearly pressing on him.

"Amanda," he started, his voice cracking as he wiped a stray tear with the back of his hand. He gave her a small, weak nod, his eyes filled with a profound sense of regret for screaming at her. "I'm sorry. I shouldn't have... I was just so scared. I know you didn't mean for this to happen. I just don't know how to fix it."

Amanda took a final step closer, the distance between them closing as the shadows of the church stretched long across the floor.

"I want to help you," she said softly, her voice steady and filled with a quiet resolve. Idris looked up, the defensive wall he usually kept up beginning to crumble, though he still looked like he expected the floor to give way at any moment.

"I believe you," Amanda continued. She tilted her head slightly, a playful, defiant grin breaking through her worry. "And honestly? I consider myself a pretty world-class judge of character. I've got a reputation to uphold, you know. I really want to prove myself right about you, just so I can tell everyone 'I told you so' later."

Idris let out a short, surprised breath, the first thing resembling a laugh she had heard from him. He looked at her, the exhaustion in his eyes softening into something warmer.

"Oh? Is that so?" he asked. The corners of his mouth lifted into a genuine, tired smile. "You're willing to bet your professional reputation on the 'boring' guy in the back pew?"

"In a heartbeat," she teased, the light, jovial tone finally shattering the heavy gloom of the sanctuary.

Amanda's smile faltered, the jovial air between them suddenly feeling thin and fragile. She knelt on the wooden kneeler beside him, not as a journalist hunting for a lead, but as someone trying to anchor a sinking ship.

"I need the whole story though, Idris," she said, her voice dropping to a serious, steady whisper. "If I'm going to help you, I can't be fighting in the dark. I need to know what we're actually up against."

Idris's smile vanished as quickly as it had appeared. He pulled back, his gaze fixating on the flickering votive candles as if he could see ghosts in the flames.

"I don't want you to help me," he replied, his voice flat and cold with a sudden, protective distance. He looked at her then, and the fear in his eyes wasn't for himself, it was for her. "Everyone who wants to help me seems to disappear or end up dead, Amanda. My friends... they tried to look into it. And now they're gone."

He shook his head, his hands clenching into white-knuckled fists on his lap. "I don't want to have that happen to anyone else. Especially not you. The headline today? That's nothing compared to what they do to people who actually get close to the truth. If you stay near me, you're just putting a target on your back."

The silence that followed was heavy. The "safe space" of the church suddenly felt smaller, the thick stone walls no longer feeling like a fortress, but like a place where secrets were buried.

Amanda didn't flinch. She didn't pull away or look frightened by his warning. Instead, she sat down on the hard wooden bench, turning fully toward him.

"The 'target' is already there, Idris," she said, her voice dropping all the softness from before. She pointed toward the heavy doors they both knew led back to a village filled with Alyssa's newspapers. "That headline didn't just ruin your life; it linked me to you. Everyone saw me run after you. Everyone knows I left the lab in a panic. If these people you're afraid of are as smart as you say, they already know I'm the only one in this town who actually cares enough to hide you."

Idris opened his mouth to protest, but she cut him off with a sharp wave of her hand.

"You think you're protecting me by keeping me ignorant? You're not. You're just making me a blind target. If I don't know who is coming or what they want, I can't protect myself, and I certainly can't protect you. You want me to stay safe? Then give me the map. Tell me exactly what happened in Baltis."

She leaned in, her gaze locked onto his with a fierce, stubborn intensity that matched his own.

"I'm not leaving this pew, and I'm not letting you walk out those doors until I know what we're fighting. You don't get to decide my risks for me, Idris. Now, start from the beginning. What is the actual story?"

Idris stared at the floor, his breath hitching as he realized Amanda wasn't going to back down. He saw the fire in her eyes, the same stubbornness that had made her the editor-in-chief, and he knew he couldn't protect her through silence anymore.

He reached into his pocket and pulled out a small, battered USB drive, rolling it between his fingers as if it were a live grenade. And thus he started his story.

"It started at Baltis University," Idris began, his voice sounding hollow against the church stone. "I had a friend named Zach, short for Zechariah. We'd been inseparable since we were kids, but our lives looked very different on paper. My parents owned a pharmaceutical firm; I grew up with every advantage, never having to worry about the cost of a degree. But Zach's father worked for Lumivox Inc., and the pay was barely enough to keep them afloat."

He looked down at his hands, his fingers ghosting over an imaginary keyboard. "To help his family make ends meet, Zach took a part-time job at Lumivox as well. People called me a whiz, a prodigy... but the truth was, Zach was the genius. He was miles better than I ever was. While I had a talent for building clean, structured code, Zach had a mind that moved at a frequency I couldn't even touch. He lived for the hunt, hacking into systems just to see if he could find the one loose thread that would make the whole thing unravel."

Idris's expression darkened. "Because he was working in the billing department, he noticed something he wasn't supposed to. He came across internal ledgers showing that Lumivox was earning far more than it was reporting, and certainly more than it was paying its struggling employees. He found a massive, recurring trail of money being siphoned off and sent to a hidden offshore account. Driven by the unfairness of his father's measly paycheck, Zach decided to look into where that money was actually going. And because I was his best friend, I started helping him stitch the pieces together. I was just the architect following his lead, but we didn't realize we were weaving our own nooses.

"We found out that massive payments were being made to various government employees and to a lot of politicians.

"It wasn't just about the money," Idris said, his voice dropping to a jagged whisper. "It was what those bribes were buying. We found evidence that Lumivox was paying off officials to look the other way while they dumped toxic waste illegally, poisoning the very land people lived on. They were buying votes to pass legislation that benefited only them and rigged every government tender so that no competitor ever stood a chance. They owned the market because they had bought the rules."

He leaned closer, the candlelight reflecting the fear in his eyes. "But the money was only half of it. The other half was far darker. Zach found a 'black file', a digital vault of secrets. Lumivox had been harvesting the private lives of the most powerful people in the country. They had everything: hidden bank accounts, illicit affairs, private medical records. They were holding the elite for ransom. If a politician didn't take the bribe, they'd simply leak a file that would ruin their life."

Idris shook his head. "They weren't just a corporation anymore. They were a shadow government. And Zach and I were the only two people who had the key to their vault."

"Zach was more driven by the fact that his father was being taken advantage of than by the thrill of the hack," Idris said, his gaze fixed on the flickering shadows of the church. "He watched his dad leave for work before the sun was up and crawl back home long after it had set, looking older and more broken every single day. Zach barely saw him. And yet, for all that sacrifice, the wages they provided didn't even begin to measure up to the labor he was putting in."

He paused, a bitter edge creeping into his voice. "Seeing his father's life being drained away for a paycheck that couldn't even cover the bills... it did something to Zach. It turned his genius into a weapon. He didn't just want to see the money; he wanted to know why his father was being treated like a disposable part in a machine that was clearly overflowing with wealth. He wanted to see who was sitting at the top while the people at the bottom were starving."

"I was just the one who helped him sharpen the blade," Idris whispered. "But once he started digging and found those bribes, the illegal dumping, the rigged tenders, the blackmail, it wasn't just about his father's wages anymore. It was about the fact that Lumivox owned everyone."

"I'll admit it. At first, I didn't want to get involved," Idris said, his voice dropping to a somber, reflective tone. "I was comfortable. I had a future. I told Zach we should just leave it alone, that it was too dangerous. But Zach wouldn't stop, and eventually, he dragged me out of the lab and into the real world. He closed his eyes, as if the images were burned into his eyelids. "We met families whose children were suffering from chronic illnesses that no one could explain, and elderly workers paralyzed by the stress of being exploited until they had nothing left. We saw the medical bills they couldn't pay and the grief in their eyes. Once I saw those faces, once I realized the actual human cost of those rigged tenders and illegal dumping, I couldn't look away anymore. My conscience wouldn't let me."

"That was the moment the 'boring coder' died," Idris whispered, looking up at Amanda. "We stopped just looking at data and started gathering evidence to give those people their lives back. But Lumivox doesn't value human lives. They only value the secret."

"We thought we were doing the right thing," Idris said, a bitter, jagged edge to his voice. "We took the evidence, the spreadsheets, the blackmail files, the toxic waste reports, straight to the police station. But the 'big guns' there wouldn't even let us past the front desk. They looked at our files, looked at us, and then looked at the clock. They refused to take a statement, claiming our 'evidence' was nothing more than high-tech fairy tales. We realized then that the rot didn't stop at the Lumivox gates; it was sitting in the captain's office, too."

He took a sharp, shaky breath, his chest tightening as he reached the hardest part of the story.

"We were desperate. We were being watched, and we knew it. So, we turned to Jordan," Idris whispered. He gripped the edge of the pew so hard his knuckles turned a ghostly white. "I knew him from back home. Mrs. Potts was my mother's closest friend, and I'd grown up seeing him as the older brother who actually made it out. But Jordan was headstrong. That's why he'd transferred himself to the Baltis precinct in the first place."

He took a sharp, shaky breath. "He was a rookie policeman, the only one in that whole precinct who actually seemed to remember the oath he took. He was the only person who would listen. He promised to help us out and also was willing to come along with us to bring those scoundrels to justice. He said if he got the necessary information, he'd bypass his superiors and take it straight to the federal level. He thought his badge made him untouchable."

Idris's eyes filled with a sudden, fresh wave of grief. "We thought he was our shield because of that history, because he was one of us. We didn't realize that by trusting a rookie, we were just handing a death sentence to the only honest man in the city. We were leading him straight into the slaughter."

Idris's voice dropped to a hollow, haunted whisper. "A few days after we gave him the files, it happened. They didn't come for him with guns in a dark alley; that would have been too obvious. Instead, he supposedly died in a catastrophic car failure. They said his brakes failed on the highway, sending him off the road. The impact caused a fuel leak, and he was... he was engulfed in flames before anyone could reach him."

He squeezed his eyes shut, the orange glow of the church's votive candles likely looking too much like the fire he was describing. "There was nothing left of the car, and certainly nothing left of the evidence we'd given him. To the world, it was just a tragic accident involving a young officer. But I saw the logs. I saw the remote-access pings to his car's internal computer system just minutes before the crash."

"That's when I realized," Idris said, looking at Amanda with a chilling clarity. "They don't just kill you. They erase the 'why.' They made it all look like an accident."

As the story poured out of him, Amanda didn't interrupt. She didn't reach for a notepad or check her phone for the time; she simply sat in the heavy silence of the church, her presence a quiet anchor against the storm of his memories. She watched the way his hands shook and the way he looked at the flickering candles as if they were the very flames that had claimed Jordan.

Her heart ached with a profound, visceral compassion. She realized that for months, Idris had been carrying a graveyard inside him, haunted by the "what ifs" and the faces of the friends he couldn't save. Every time he faltered or his voice broke, she leaned in closer, her expression softening into one of deep, empathetic understanding. There was no judgment in her eyes, only a fierce, protective warmth that seemed to say you don't have to carry this alone anymore.

When he finally spoke of the fire that took Jordan, Amanda instinctively reached out, resting a gentle hand on his trembling shoulder. It was a simple gesture, but in the hollow cold of the sanctuary, it felt like a lifeline. She wasn't just listening to a "scoop", she was witnessing the shattering of a human soul, and her only instinct was to help him put the pieces back together.

Idris' breadth hitched and Amanda gently encouraged him to continue his story.

"After we found out he was murdered, that his life had been snuffed out and then doctored into a tragic 'accident', everything changed for me," Idris said, his voice dropping to a low, dangerous vibration. "The fear was gone, replaced by a cold, burning need to bring those idiots to justice. I didn't care about the risks anymore. I wanted them to pay for Jordan."

He looked away, a shadow of guilt crossing his face. "But Zach... the fire changed him. He was a genius, but he was still just a kid who wanted to help his dad. Seeing what happened to Jordan broke his spirit. He was terrified; he wanted to give up, to delete everything and disappear because he valued his life more than a cause that seemed to be killing everyone we touched. He begged me to stop."

"But I couldn't," Idris whispered, his eyes reflecting the haunting weight of that choice. "I was blinded by the need for justice. I thought if we just pushed a little harder, if we found just one more link, the truth would be too big for them to bury. I didn't realize that by dragging a scared friend deeper into the lion's den, I was making the same mistake I made with Jordan. I was valuing the truth more than the people I loved."

Amanda watched him, her heart heavy with the realization that Idris's "killer" reputation wasn't just a lie told by a corporation, it was a label he had used to punish himself for his own stubbornness.

"There were cracks in our friendship after that," Idris admitted, his voice barely a thread. "The arguments got louder, the silences longer. Zach wanted out, and I wanted blood. But in the end, Zach stayed. He came through for me because of our history, because he couldn't leave me to face that monster alone. He chose loyalty over his own safety."

He stopped, his breathing suddenly becoming shallow and unregulated, his chest heaving as if the air in the church had turned to lead. Amanda moved closer, her presence the only thing keeping him from spiraling.

"And then that day came," he choked out. "Zach didn't show up for our meeting. His father called me, frantic, Zach had been reported missing. I was the last person anyone had seen him with. A few hours later, they found a body. It was... they said it was burned beyond recognition, just like Jordan. They labeled it as his immediately. They refused to let his father see him, refused to let any independent examiners near the morgue. They just closed the case and pointed their fingers at me."

He wiped a frantic hand across his face. "I was taken in as the main suspect. The only reason I'm not in a cell right now is because they couldn't find a motive, and I had hard video footage of myself at home during the time of the death. But Lumivox didn't care about the law. They used their reach to push a different story, a 'friendship gone wrong.' They leaked a narrative that I was a jealous, wealthy kid who snapped when his friend tried to walk away. They didn't need a judge to sentence me. They just made sure the world called me a murderer until I had nowhere left to go."

"I couldn't go anywhere in Baltis," Idris whispered, his voice heavy with the memory of a thousand judging glares. "It didn't matter that there was no trial. In the eyes of the public, I was a monster. Every time I stepped outside, people looked at me with those weird, hollow eyes, some with fear, others with pure hatred. I was a murderer to all of them, a ghost walking through my own life."

He looked up at the rafters of the church, a small, sad smile touching his lips when he mentioned his family. "My parents... they were the only ones who stayed. They were super supportive through the whole nightmare, but they saw what it was doing to me. They knew I was drowning in that city. They were the ones who pushed me to leave, who found this place and helped me move here. They thought if I could just get far enough away, the noise would stop."

He let out a jagged breath, looking at Amanda with a raw vulnerability. "For a while, it worked. I was just the quiet guy in the back of the class again. Until that headline hit the college site today. Now, it feels like the water is rising all over again."

Idris showed the USB drive to Amanda.

"You probably why they haven't just finished me off like they did Jordan and Zach," Idris said, his voice dropping to a low, cold vibration. He held the drive up to the candlelight. "This is the only reason I'm still breathing. It's a decryption key. I've built a 'Dead Man's Switch' into a series of encrypted cloud servers. If I don't log in and enter a specific sequence every twenty-four hours, the 'Black Files', the bribes, the murders, the names of every politician Lumivox owns, get sent to every major news desk from London to New York."

He looked at Amanda, the flickering orange light making his eyes look hollow. "They know exactly where I am, Amanda. But they can't touch me physically without risking total exposure. They aren't trying to kill my body; they're killing my credibility. If they turn the whole world against me, if they make me look like a mentally unstable murderer, then even when the files leak, no one will believe the source. They're trying to isolate me until they can find a way to hack my server and kill the switch. Once that happens, I'm a dead man."

He gripped the drive tighter. "By coming here today, by linking yourself to me in that newspaper, you didn't just join a local scandal. You stepped into the middle of a digital standoff. And I don't know how much longer my code can hold them back."

Amanda didn't hesitate. Seeing the way Idris's shoulders finally slumped, as if the literal weight of the Baltis tragedy had been lifted by the act of sharing it, she reached out and pulled him into a firm, grounding hug. He was shivering, his breath still hitching in the cold air of the nave, but as she held him, the tremors began to subside. She realized then that this wasn't just about a secret; it was about the toll of a long, lonely exile. Telling her had finally allowed him to breathe.

As they sat back, the silence of the church no longer felt like a hiding place, but a war room. Amanda's journalist instincts kicked into high gear, but this time, she wasn't hunting a scoop, she was protecting a friend.

She pulled out her phone, the screen's glow sharp against the dim stone walls. She quickly pulled up the Church WhatsApp group, scrolling until she found Belinda's number.

"I found him," she typed, her fingers flying across the glass. "He's with me and he's safe. Please don't worry."

"Frank, get the others together. Meet me at the coffee shop in twenty minutes. We have a lot to talk about, and we need to move fast."

Amanda looked at Idris, her gaze steady despite the heavy silence of the church. Part of her wanted to keep him here, tucked away in the quiet safety of the rafters where the world couldn't reach him. But the strategist in her knew that a secret shared was a secret protected.

"Idris, look at me," she said, her voice a calm anchor. He finally met her eyes, though his breath was still shallow. "I've spent my career finding people I can trust, and I'm telling you, the people I just messaged are the best I know. She held out her hand, not as a journalist demanding a story, but as a friend offering a bridge. "Trust my judgment one more time. Let me show you what it feels like to have people in your corner who can't be bought."

Idris stared at her hand for a long moment, the flickering candlelight reflecting the battle between his fear and his need for a home. Finally, he reached out, his fingers trembling as they closed around hers.

"Okay," he whispered, standing up and smoothing his clothes as if armoring himself for the world outside. "Let's go."
